PASSAGE P5: Closing the Sea Arms
=====================================================

[1] On the night of January 31, 1953, a storm drove the North Sea against the southwestern coast of the Netherlands. The wind arrived together with a spring tide, and the water rose higher than anyone living had seen. Before dawn, the sea had broken through the dikes in dozens of places. By the time it retreated, 1,836 people had drowned, and tens of thousands had lost their homes.

[2] The disaster did not come as a complete surprise. For years an engineer named Johan van Veen had warned that many dikes were to low and too weak. He had even sketched a solution: instead of raising hundreds of miles of dikes, the country could close off the sea arms of the southwestern delta with a few enormous dams, shortening the coastline that had to be defended. Before 1953, his plan seemed too expensive, within three weeks of the flood, the government had formed a commission to carry it out.

[3] The work, called the Delta Works, took more than forty years. The most famous structure, the Eastern Scheldt barrier, was originally meant to be a solid dam. But fishermen and environmentalists objected. A solid dam would turn the estuary behind it into a still lake and destroy the oyster and mussel beds that depended from the tides. After years of debate, engineers designed something new: a barrier of sixty-two steel gates, left open in normal weather and lowered only when a storm threatens. Finished in 1986, it cost far more than a dam but allowed the tides to keep flowing.

[4] The last major piece, a barrier on the waterway leading to Rotterdam was completed in 1997. Its two hinged arms, each nearly as long as the Eiffel Tower is tall, rests in dry docks on the banks. They swing out to close the channel only when the water is forecast to rise dangerously.

[5] Today engineers from other low-lying countries visit the delta to study these structures.

PASSAGE P6: Music from the Tower
=====================================================

[1] High in the bell tower of St. Rumbold's Cathedral in Mechelen, Belgium, a musician sits at an instrument that looks like a cross between an organ and a loom. Instead of keys, it has two rows of wooden handles called batons, and a set of pedals below. The musician plays by striking the batons with loosely closed fists. Each baton is connected by a wire to a clapper inside one of the bronze bells hanging overhead, and the sound carries across the whole city.

[2] This instrument is a carillon, and it must have at least twenty-three bells. Carillons first appeared in the towns of Flanders and the Netherlands more than five hundred years ago. In the end, town bells were used to mark the hours, and a few notes were played before the hour struck to warn people to listen. Over time the tunes grew longer, and towns added more bells to play them.

[3] Bells are difficult to tune. A bell sounds not one pitch but several at once, and if those pitches clash, a melody played on it will sound sour. In the 1600s two brothers, François and Pieter Hemony, have learned to shave metal from the inside of a bell on a lathe until its pitches agreed. Their bells were so fragile that many still hang in Dutch towers today.

[4] The carillon owes much of its modern revival to Mechelen. Around 1900 the city's carillonneur, Jef Denyn, improved the mechanism so that a player could control how softly or loudly each bell sounded. His evening concerts drew crowds of thousands into the square below the tower. In 1922 he founded the world's first school for carillon players, and students still travel there from around the world.

[5] Playing a carillon has minor challenges. Each baton, the player must remember, pulls a wire attached to a clapper. The musician sits in a small cabin directly below the bells, where the sound is so loud, and so oddly balanced, that it is hard to judge what listeners on the ground are hearing. And unlike a piano, a carillon has no way to silence a note. Once struck, a large bell may ring for half a minute, every note a player strikes stays in the air long after the next has begun.

PASSAGE P7: A Clock Carved from Wood
=====================================================

[1] In the early 1750s, a young tobacco farmer in Maryland borrowed a pocket watch and took it apart. Benjamin Banneker had never seen a clock up close, but he studied the watch's gears until he understood how they worked. Then, using a pocketknife, larger versions of each gear were carved out of wood. The striking clock he built kept time for the rest of his life.

[2] Banneker's clock made him famous throughout Maryland. His grandmother taught him to read from a Bible, and he attended a small country school for a few winters. In contrast, he taught himself, borrowing books whenever he could. He was especially drawn to mathematics, and he liked to solve the puzzles that travelers brought him.

[3] When Banneker was nearly sixty, a neighbor named George Ellicott lent him several books on astronomy and a telescope. Banneker sat up nights in an orchard watching the stars within a year he could predict eclipses. In 1791 George's cousin Andrew Ellicott, a surveyor hired him to help lay out the boundaries of the new federal capital. For three months Banneker kept the expedition's astronomical clock and recorded the positions of stars, from which the surveyors fixed their location.

[4] That same year Banneker completed an almanac, a yearly book of: tide tables, weather predictions, and the times of sunrise and sunset. He mailed the handwritten pages to Thomas Jefferson, then the secretary of state, with a long letter. Jefferson had written that Black people were less capable than white people. Banneker's letter politely reminded him of the words he had written in the Declaration of Independence, that all men are created equal, and offered the almanac as evidence against his opinion.

[5] Jefferson replied within weeks and forwarded the almanac to a scientific academy in Paris. Banneker published his almanacs every year until 1797, and it sold in several states.
