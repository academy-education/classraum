For centuries, the Dutch protected their low, flat country from rivers in the same way: by building dikes, long walls of earth that hold water back. When a flood broke through, people built the dikes higher. Each time, the taller walls seemed to solve the problem.

In 1995, that confidence was shaken. Heavy rain sent the Rhine and Meuse rivers surging, and about 250,000 people were told to leave their homes. The dikes held, but only barely. The old dikes still stand along most rivers. But now, when the water rises, it has places to go.

## Item 10

Strand: Analyze Central Idea, Concepts, and Events; Summarize | Grade target: 7 | Target band: RIT 200-209

Passage:

Before the 1880s, each American town set its clocks by the sun. When the sun stood highest in the sky, it was noon. Because the sun appears to move from east to west, noon in Boston came about twelve minutes earlier than noon in New York.

For most people, this caused few problems. A farmer rarely traveled far enough in a day to notice. But the railroads changed that. A train crossing several states passed through dozens of local times, and each railroad printed its schedules using its own choice of clock. One station in Pittsburgh is said to have shown six different times. Travelers missed their connections, and trains sharing the same track risked crashing into each other.

In 1883, the railroads took matters into their own hands. They divided the country into four time zones, and on November 18, stations reset their clocks to match. Some towns grumbled about "railroad time" and kept the sun's time for a while. Yet within a few years, most of them had switched.

Congress did not make the zones official until 1918, thirty-five years after the railroads first used them.

B. Time zones made the first railroads possible, since trains could not run safely until all the clocks in the country matched.
C. Many towns would not give up sun time, so the railroads had to run on dozens of local times for years.
D. Trains made each town's sun time hard to follow, so the railroads set up time zones that the nation later took up.

In a dry valley in the western United States, a small creek used to vanish every summer. By August, its bed was cracked mud, and the meadows around it turned brown.

Scientists thought beavers could help. But the creek had too little wood and too few deep pools for beavers to settle there. So the scientists pounded rows of wooden posts across the creek and wove willow branches between them. The posts slowed the water just enough to form small ponds.

Within a year, beavers moved in and began building real dams on top of the posts. The ponds behind the dams spread water out into the meadows. The soaked ground acted like a sponge, slowly letting water seep back into the creek for months. Now the creek flows even in late summer, and the meadows stay green.

Not everyone was pleased at first. Some ranchers worried that the ponds would flood nearby roads. The scientists chose spots away from the roads, and over time the complaints died down.

C. Scientists set posts in the creek so beavers would move in. The beavers' dams held the water back in ponds, so the meadows filled up while the creek ran dry.
D. Beavers built dams that slowed the creek. Then scientists added posts to make the dams stronger, and the stronger dams kept the creek from flooding the roads.
