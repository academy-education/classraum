PASSAGE P9: Borrowing a Ladder
=====================================================

[1] Our public library lends more than books. Since last spring, card holders have been able to borrow a pressure washer, a sewing machine, a telescope, or any of about two hundred other objects from the new "library of things" in the basement. The collection began with donations and a small grant, and it has been popular from the first week, the waiting list for the carpet cleaner is currently eleven names long.

[2] The county board will decide next month whether to give the program permanent funding. The vote is scheduled for the board's public meeting on the fourteenth. We urge it to say yes.

[3] The case is partly about money. Most households use a drill for a few minutes a year, and an infrequently used tool costs far more to own than to borrow. A family that borrows a tent for one camping trip save the cost of buying one and the space of storing it. Multiply those savings across a county of 90,000 people, and the program's extravagant budget looks like a bargain.

[4] The case is also about what a library is for. Libraries began as a way to share things that were too expensive for most people to own alone; in the nineteenth century, that meant books. Today a book is cheap, but a ladder long enough to reach a second-story gutter is not. Nevertheless, lending one fits the library's original purpose as well as lending a novel does.

[5] Critics argue that borrowed tools will come back broken. The library's records insinuate otherwise. Of more than 1,400 loans so far, staff have recorded less than twenty items returned damaged, and every one was repaired for less than the cost of a single new tool. The board should fund the program and let the waiting list for the carpet cleaner get shorter.

PASSAGE P10: The Fossil Hunter
=====================================================

[1] The cliffs near Lyme Regis, on the southern coast of England, are made of soft gray rock and clay that crumble in winter storms. Each landslide exposes the remains of animals that lived in the sea some 200 million years ago. In the early 1800s, few people searched those cliffs as carefully, or as successfully, as Mary Anning.

[2] [A] Anning was born in 1799 into a poor family. [B] Her father, a cabinetmaker, collected fossils from the beach and sold them to tourists, and he often took his children along. [C] When he died in 1810, he left the family in debt, and fossil hunting became one of the ways the Anning's paid their bills. [D]

[3] Around 1811, Mary's brother Joseph found a strange skull in the cliffs. Over the following months, Mary, then about twelve, uncovered the rest of the skeleton; a creature with a long snout and paddle-like limbs that scientists later named Ichthyosaurus, or "fish lizard." In 1823 she found the first complete skeleton of a plesiosaur, a marine reptile with a tiny head on an extraordinarily long neck. The find was so unusual as a leading French anatomist at first suspected it was a fake.

[4] Anning also noticed that certain stones found inside skeletons contains fish bones and scales, and she suggested that the stones were fossilized droppings. Scientists bought her specimens and drew on her observations, but they rarely named her when they published. As a woman, she was banished from the Geological Society of London; she could not even attend its meetings.

[5] Recognition came slowly. When she died in 1847, the society's president delivered a tribute to her, an unusual honor for someone who had never been allowed to join. Today several of her finds stand in the Natural History Museum in London, and the labels beside them carry her name.

PASSAGE P11: Stitched History
=====================================================

[1] The Bayeux Tapestry is not, strictly speaking, a tapestry. A true tapestry is woven, with the design built into the fabric thread by thread. The Bayeux work was embroidered, its scenes were stitched in colored wool onto long strips of plain linen. The name stuck anyway, and after more than nine centuries no one seems inclined to change it.

[2] The embroidery is about half a meter tall and nearly seventy meters long. Scholars have counted more than six hundred human figures in its scenes, along with nearly two hundred horses and dozens of ships. It tells the story of the Norman conquest of England in 1066, from an oath that the English nobleman Harold supposedly swore to William, Duke of Normandy, to the Battle of Hastings, where Harold was killed. Short Latin captions names the people and places.

[3] Historians believe that the work was commissioned by William's half-brother, Bishop Odo, and it being probably made by English embroiderers within a few years of the battle. That origin makes it an ordinary document: a celebration of the Norman victory, stitched by people of the defeated nation.

[4] The main scenes are infested with detail. For instance, ships are loaded with wine barrels and horses, and soldiers wear shirts of chain mail. Above and below the story run two narrow borders filled with lions, birds, and scenes from fables. In one panel a comet streaks across the sky, a sight that observers in 1066 took as an omen, it is now thought to have been Halley's comet.

[5] The final section of the embroidery is missing, and no one knows how the story originally ended. Perhaps the last panels showed William's coronation. What survives, however, is remarkable enough. For anyone who wants to know how people in the eleventh century fought, dressed, and sailed, few sources are as vivid.

PASSAGE P12: The Day of Two Noons
=====================================================

[1] Before 1883, nearly every town in the United States kept its own time. Noon was the moment the sun stood loftiest in the sky, and because the sun appears to move westward, noon in Boston came several minutes before noon in a town to its west. For most of history, this hardly mattered. In contrast, a farmer or a shopkeeper rarely needed to know what time it was two hundred miles away.

[2] The railroads changed that. A train leaving Chicago passed through dozens of local times on its way east, and each railroad kept their own schedule, usually set by the clock in its headquarters city. Stations sometimes displayed several clocks, one for each railroad that stopped there, and all of them were different. Travelers missed connections, and the risk of collisions on single-track lines were growing.

[3] The solution came not from the government but from the railroads themselves. At a meeting in 1883, railroad managers agreed to divide the country into four time zones, each one hour apart. Within each zone, every clock would show the same time.

[4] The change took effect at noon on November 18, 1883. In cities across the country, jewelers and telegraph operators reset their clocks as the signal arrived. Towns in the eastern part of each zone, set their clocks back and so experienced noon twice that day. Newspapers called it "the day of two noons."

[5] Not everyone was pleased. Some towns refused to give up "God's time," as they called solar time, and kept their old clocks for years. Yet within a generation, standard time had become so ordinary that most people forgot it had ever been an invention. Congress did not make the time zones law until 1918, by that time it was simply confirming what the country had been doing for thirty-five years.
