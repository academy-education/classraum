## Item 1

Passage:

The printing press is often praised for teaching Europe to read, as if cheaper books alone made people readers. The timeline does not support that story. For more than a hundred years, printers mostly served people who could already read, such as priests, lawyers, and merchants. Most early books were church texts and scholarly works in Latin, a language most Europeans could not read at all. In many regions, reading rates barely changed until much later, when church campaigns and school programs gave ordinary people a reason to read and lessons in how to do it. Printing made those later efforts cheaper. On its own, though, it supplied neither the reason nor the teaching.

A. Where printing came early, reading rates rose sharply only many years later, once church schools opened.
B. Reading rates rose fastest in the first years after printing began, well before any schools opened.
C. Across Europe, the number of books grew many times over within a few short decades of the first printing.
D. Early printers fought hard with one another to sell Latin copies of old Greek and Roman books.

Passage:

It sounds strange that a warming planet could make part of Europe colder, but the ocean explains how. A huge Atlantic current carries warm water from the tropics north toward Europe. The current is driven partly by cold, salty water sinking in the far North Atlantic. As Greenland's ice melts, it pours fresh water into that area. Fresh water is lighter, so it does not sink as easily. The current then slows, and less tropical heat reaches northwestern Europe. Scientists are careful about this claim. They do not predict a new ice age, and they agree that the planet as a whole keeps warming. They say only that a warmer world could still produce a cooler northwestern Europe.

B. Greenland's ice sheet lost ice faster in warmer years, and more of it melted off its edges during each hot summer.
C. As more fresh water flowed into the North Atlantic, deep water sank more slowly and less heat moved north.
D. Ocean water in the tropics warmed at the same pace as the world as a whole did.

Most histories say Japan built factories quickly after 1868 for one reason. Its new Meiji government brought in Western machines and foreign advisers. The machines mattered, but they do not explain why Japan could put them to use when many other countries could not. Long before 1868, Japan already had high literacy, busy trade networks, and skilled village workshops. Its merchant houses kept careful account books. So the new machines met workers and managers who were ready to run them. Foreign experts were sent home as soon as Japanese workers could replace them, often within ten years. Japan's success came less from borrowing than from business skills it already had.

The "hygiene hypothesis" is often told as a simple warning. Modern homes are so clean that children meet few germs. As a result, their immune systems never learn properly, and allergies follow. Most immune scientists no longer accept that version. The rise in allergies does not match up well with fewer common childhood illnesses, such as colds and flu. Instead, it matches up with less contact with certain ancient, mostly harmless microbes. These "old friends" evolved alongside humans and help tune the immune system. In this newer view, a childhood full of colds gives little protection, while contact with the right microbes from the environment does.

Many people believe that time together makes rival groups less prejudiced. Decades of studies show that contact often does reduce bias. But the idea is not that simple. Early researchers said contact helps only under certain conditions. The groups must meet as equals. They must work toward shared goals, with support from leaders. Without these, contact can make hostility worse. Later studies also found that some of the benefit fades once people leave the planned setting. So the evidence supports a narrower claim. Carefully planned contact reduces prejudice. Simply being around another group will not do the job by itself.

Microloans are small loans for people whom banks turn away. People once hoped these loans would lift families out of poverty by helping them start businesses. Careful studies have cooled that hope. Most borrowers did not earn more, and their businesses rarely grew past a very small size. But the studies agree on a quieter benefit. The loans helped families with the timing of their spending. Families could get through lean months and pay for big, one-time costs they could not have managed otherwise. Microloans, the evidence suggests, do not create new wealth. Instead, they help families spread the money they already have across time.

## Item 8

Passage:

In the famous "marshmallow test," young children could eat one treat now or wait for two treats later. Those who waited did better later in life. Many took this as proof that self-control is a fixed trait. Some researchers now question that lesson. Waiting only makes sense if a promise can be trusted. A child who has learned that adults often break promises is being sensible by taking the sure treat. When experimenters first showed they were reliable, children waited much longer. When they proved unreliable, even "patient" children grabbed the treat at once. The test may measure not willpower but whether a child expects promises to be kept. That depends on how stable the child's home has been.

Question: Which finding, if true, would most support the researchers' new view of the test?

A. Longer waiters were more responsible years later, even counting how stable their homes were.
B. Children from homes often short on food waited less, despite equal self-control test scores.
C. Children waited just as long after seeing a promise broken as after seeing one kept.
D. Older children waited longer than younger ones, and the gap grew with each year.

When photos showed narrow gullies on the walls of craters on Mars, many people thought flowing water had carved them. If so, Mars might once have been friendly to life. They are also most active in the middle of winter, not in summer. That is when frozen carbon dioxide, called dry ice, builds up and then turns straight into gas. So many scientists now think the gullies were carved by blocks of dry ice sliding on the gas they give off.

Scientists once marked a small fish called a cleaner wrasse with a colored spot. After the fish saw itself in a mirror, it scraped its body against rocks. Passing this mirror test may only show that an animal can link a reflection to its own body. That does not require any idea of a self. The wrasse's way of life gives a simpler answer. This fish gets its food by finding spots on other fish and picking them off. So it may be ready to react to a spot wherever it sees one, even in a mirror.

## Item 11

Passage:

Wolves were brought back to Yellowstone National Park. The park then became a famous example of a chain reaction in nature. With wolves back, elk stayed away from open stream banks where they could be ambushed. With less elk grazing, willow and aspen trees grew back along the streams. Newer fieldwork suggests the story is not so simple. Willows have recovered unevenly. They came back strongly where beaver dams raise the water and keep the banks wet. They did poorly where the banks stayed dry, even where wolves were clearly present and elk were clearly cautious. Wolves may be needed for the recovery, but they are not enough on their own.

Question: Which finding, if true, would most support the newer claim that the wolves' return was not enough by itself to bring the trees back?

B. Willows failed where water was low, despite many wolves living there for years.
C. Wolves caught elk most often in open valleys, where herds had few escape routes.
D. Elk numbers dropped sharply in the years right after wolves were brought back.

## Item 12

Strand: Informational Text - Analyze Central Idea, Concepts, and Events; Summarize | Grade target: 8 | Target band: RIT 210-219

Passage:

Many people see farming as an obvious upgrade. Farming made more food, so foragers gladly took it up. Ancient bones tell a different story. Early farmers were shorter than the foragers who came before them. They also had more tooth decay and more signs of poor nutrition. Farming did produce more calories per acre. But those calories came from a few starchy crops, and they cost longer hours of work. Crowded villages also spread disease. So why farm at all? Perhaps once populations had grown, foraging could no longer feed everyone. Farming may have been less a prize than a trap: good for the group's numbers, but bad for each person's health.

Question: Which finding, if true, would most weaken the author's view of the shift to farming?

A. Early crops gave many times more calories per acre than wild foods did.
B. Some forager groups next to farming villages refused to farm for many centuries.
C. Skeletons show the first farmers were taller and better fed than nearby foragers.
D. The first farming villages were bigger and more crowded than earlier forager camps.
