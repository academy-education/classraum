PASSAGE P7: Twelve Poses a Second
=====================================================

[1] On screen, a felt fox trots across a kitchen table in one easy stride that lasts about two seconds. Behind the camera, the fox is a puppet about the size of a teacup. Stop-motion animation is built from still photographs, and each photograph records a puppet that someone has nudged by hand, often by less than a millimeter. Played back quickly, the photographs blend into movement.

[2] Film is projected at twenty-four frames per second, most animators save labor by shooting on twos, holding each pose for two frames so that a second of movement needs only twelve poses. Even at that rate, the arithmetic is unforgiving. A ten-second shot requires more than a hundred adjustments, each photographed before the next can begin. Animators, which often spend days alone on a single shot, learn to think in frames rather than seconds.

[3] The puppets are built for this patience. Under the felt or silicone skin of most professional puppets is an armature. An armature is a metal skeleton whose ball-and-socket joints are stiff enough to hold any position. Small bolts called tie-downs fasten a puppets feet to the sets floor, so that the figure cannot topple while the animator reaches across it.

[4] The real difficulty is consistency. If a limb travels farther between two frames than between the previous two, the movement lurches, and viewers notice even when they cannot say why. Frame-grabbing software helps by laying a faint image of the previous frame over the camera's live view, a feature called onion skinning. However, the animator sees the old pose and the new one at the same moment and can compare them. When every increment is right, the finished motion will look smoothly and natural.

[5] All of this makes stop-motion slow even by the standards of animation. On a feature film, a single animator may finish only a few seconds of footage in a full day, and often less. Yet many directors, and a good share of their audiences, are drawn to the form for that very reason. A puppet handled thousands of times keeps faint traces of the hands that moved it: a fingerprint pressed into clay, a ripple in a felt coat. Computer animation can imitate such marks, but in stop-motion they are simply there.

PASSAGE P10: A Thousand Voices for a Nation
=====================================================

[1] A national poll may describe the opinions of an entire country, yet the firm behind it may have spoken to only a thousand people. The method, which statisticians call random sampling has been studied for generations, and it rests on a simple idea: a small group, chosen well, can stand in for a much larger one. The hard part is the choosing.

[2] Choosing well means giving every member of the population the same chance of being picked. Any rule that makes some people easier to reach than others skews the result. Nevertheless, a survey that calls only landline telephones will hear mostly from older households, since many younger adults no longer keep one. The people such a survey misses tends to differ from the ones it reaches in age, in income, and often in their views.

[3] The most famous failure of this kind came in 1936. A popular magazine mailed some ten million mock ballots to Americans whose names it had taken largely from telephone directories and lists of car owners. More than two million came back, and the magazine confidently forecast a victory for Alf Landon. Who lost to Franklin Roosevelt in one of the most lopsided elections in the nation's history. The sample's sheer size had not protected it. During the Depression, households with a telephone or a car were better off than most, and they leaned away from Roosevelt.

[4] Many modern pollsters dial numbers generated at random, so that no list decides who can be reached. Even so, some groups answer more readily than others. When a sample comes back with too few young adults, pollsters adjusted the results, counting each young respondent's answers a little more heavily than the rest. Every survey, then, requires a firm to decide whom to contact, how to reach them, and weighting the answers it gets.

[5] Every reputable poll publishes a margin of error, an estimate of how far its numbers could drift by chance alone. A larger sample narrows that margin, but by less than most people expect. Adding more people does make a poll somewhat more precise. What no amount of size can repair is a sample drawn from the wrong list, as the magazine learned in 1936, when far smaller polls named the winner correctly.

PASSAGE P11: The Plumbing Beneath Old Faithful
=====================================================

[1] Old Faithful, the best-known geyser in Yellowstone National Park, does not erupt on a fixed timetable, it keeps a rough schedule instead. Crowds still gather on the benches nearby well before each eruption, because park rangers post a predicted time at the visitor center. The prediction is usually right to within ten minutes or so, which is impressive for an event driven by water boiling deep underground. The reasons the geyser can be forecast at all, and the reasons it cannot be forecast exactly, both lie in its plumbing.

[2] Every geyser needs three things: a source of heat, a supply of water, and a narrow, twisting channel that runs from the surface down toward hot rock. Rain and melted snow seep through cracks in the ground and collect in the channel. The water at the bottom of the channel—pressed down by the weight of all the water above it—can stay liquid at temperatures well above those at which water boils at the surface.

[3] Eventually the water near the bottom grows hot enough to boil anyway. The first bubbles of steam rise and push a little water out of the vent at the top. In contrast, the column above weighs less, and the pressure on the deeper water drops. Suddenly relieved of that weight, the column above flashes into steam, and a roaring jet of water and vapor bursts from the ground. Geysers are found in only a handful of places on Earth.

[4] The length of the wait between eruptions depend mostly on how long the channel takes to refill and reheat. Rangers have learned that a long eruption, tends to be followed by a long wait. They time each eruption and use its duration, meaning how long it lasted, to forecast the next one. Most waits at Old Faithful fall somewhere between about an hour and two hours.

[5] Over longer spans, the schedule itself can shift. Minerals dissolved in the hot water slowly coat the walls of the channel and narrow it, and an earthquake many miles away can rearrange the cracks underground and change a geyser's rhythm for years. A geyser, in the end, is less like a clock than like a kettle that refills itself, and no two refills are quite the same.
