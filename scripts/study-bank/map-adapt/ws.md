## Passage for items 2-5

Strand: Informational Text | Grade target: 8 | Target band: RIT 210-219

Marta Sandoval spent thirty years compiling the first dictionary of the dialect spoken in a high mountain province. She worked at a kitchen table between teaching jobs. What happened to her finished pages is still argued about in the valleys she recorded.

No book of her own was ever bound, and she never paid a printer a single coin. The university library asked twice for her manuscript, but she still had not answered when her word cards left for the capital, and nothing ever reached the library. A newspaper offered to print one letter of the alphabet each month, and she refused to let the work come out in pieces. Then a dictionary maker in the capital borrowed her cards for a summer. Today, eleven thousand of her entries stand in his national dictionary under his name.

For nineteen years, one market day a week, she stood among the stalls asking sellers what they called things. She was sure that people speak most truly while they are haggling over goods. She began every market day with the same question: the old word for a measure of oil. She never opened church records or old wills, because she held that a word does not matter just because someone once wrote it down. The village teachers offered to have their students write to her, but she said no. By the time a child's letter reached the post office, she explained, a teacher had already corrected it. She saw no need for helpers in other villages to mail her words, either. One market drew people from twelve villages, so letters would have been a waste of time.

His three volumes never mention her name, not even in the thank-yous. Her words survived, but someone else took the credit, and nobody has yet called it what it was.

## Passage for items 11-14

Strand: Literary Text | Grade target: 6 | Target band: RIT 190-199

The gate at my aunt Odile's plant nursery sticks every August, and she has never oiled it. I got there at seven, before the sprinklers came on, and pushed it open with my shoulder. A gravel path ran from the gate to the greenhouse door.

I had come for one reason. The man who worked for her had quit on Friday, and I was there to cover his shifts. She could not pay me, and I did not ask her to. School was out until September, so I had no homework waiting for me. And I loved the old place far too much to ever want it sold.

That morning she had me letter a card for the wall above the cash register. She told me the words, and I wrote them down: NOTHING GREEN LEAVES HERE TO DIE.

At nine, a woman came in carrying a fig tree in a black pot. It was brown all the way down to the stem, and she had no receipt. She talked for a long time. My aunt carried the fig into the greenhouse to nurse it and told the woman to come back in the fall. She gave her no money from the register and no new tree, and the woman went home with empty hands. "Nothing alive gets thrown out of this shop," my aunt said. We had not even grown that fig ourselves, and that made no difference to her. Receipt or no receipt, story or no story, she would have done exactly the same thing.

I locked the gate at six. At breakfast, I had told my mother just what my aunt would do with a customer like that. I had been unkind about her, and I had been wrong. Now I could see why she had done each thing, and she had been right about every bit of it. The person I felt bad about now was me. On the bus home I did not smile once, and my face went hot every time I remembered breakfast.

## Passage for items 15-18

Strand: Literary Text | Grade target: 7 | Target band: RIT 200-209

Kellerman's boatyard closes for good on the first of September, and I spent the last week of August there with my uncle Teodor. His boat, the Marisol, sat on blocks in the shed. My grandfather built her, and Teodor ran her to the island and back for half his life.

By Monday, every family on the lake had an idea for what to do with her. By Friday, she had a new owner. The sailing club had asked in June to use her for teaching beginners. "A gift pays no debt," he told them, and no beginner ever stepped aboard. Petrus rowed over to offer his small boat and motor in a trade. Teodor could still handle the Marisol by himself, and he said a trade brings no money through the gate. Back in June, he had pushed a knife into her planks near the keel. The blade would not sink in even a quarter inch, and there was no rot anywhere. In the end, the marina at Two Harbours sent a check, and he signed it over to the bank.

The bank had held a loan on the yard since the bad winter, and the amount owed had grown larger than a whole season of repairs could earn. Every other plan that week hit the same wall: pay in September, or the doors would never open again. "The yard will outlast any boat in it," he said. "I will not close these doors to keep one hull in the family."

The sale drew half the people on the lake to the shed on Friday, and I carried out chairs for strangers. I had argued against the sale all week. When a neighbor told me my uncle had done the brave thing, I walked away without a word. I was furious with him all evening. I kept bringing it up at dinner long after he had stopped answering, and my jaw stayed tight the whole night. Even then, I knew it was not fair to blame him. That check was the yard's only chance.
