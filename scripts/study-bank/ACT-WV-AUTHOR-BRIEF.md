# ACT Reading, whole-passage variants: author brief

You are writing ONE ACT (enhanced ACT, 2025-) Reading unit: **four complete versions of one
passage** and **one fixed set of nine four-choice questions**. With this method **nobody,
including you, knows which version students will read.**

## The method

- You write four complete passages (v0..v3) on the same topic, in the same genre, voice and
  length. They share the same people, setting and general arc, and differ in what actually
  happened, what was believed, what was decided, why, and how the writer regards it.
- You write one set of nine questions. Each has four choices (c0..c3), and the same stems and
  choices are used with every version.
- **In version k, choice k is the correct answer to every question.** v0 makes c0 correct for
  all nine questions, v1 makes c1 correct for all nine, and so on. So each version is one coherent
  "world" in which the nine c_k answers are all true together. Design the four worlds first, and
  only then write the prose.
- After all units are frozen, a hash-seeded draw picks ONE version per unit, and that is what
  students read. **No version is "the real one."** All four must be equally natural, complete and
  well written; a weak version has a 1-in-4 chance of being the one shown.

## The goal: items that make a strong reader THINK

Earlier rounds of this method produced natural prose but EASY items, for two reasons, and both
are forbidden here:

1. **A wrong option named something the shown passage never mentions.** A reader just struck the
   option that was not in the text.
2. **The passage flatly dismissed the wrong options** ("relief was the wrong word"; "the earlier
   tests failed"). A reader struck whatever the passage waved away.

There was also a pattern ACROSS questions: the right answer was always "what actually happened",
never something believed, planned or implied.

### A. Every option is about something the passage actually discusses, in EVERY version

- For every question except vocabulary and attitude, **each of the four options must concern
  people, things or events that are present in each of the four versions.** That includes the
  right answer and all three wrong answers, in every version.
- Use the option's own distinguishing words (or plain forms of them: *sell / sold / sale*) in the
  passage, so a reader recognises what the option is talking about. If an option says "a loan
  from the harbor board", the harbor board and the loan are in every version.
- So **no option can be struck because "the passage never mentions that."** A machine check
  confirms this across all four versions.

### B. Wrong options are tempting PARTIAL readings, never things the passage throws away

Make each wrong option something a careful-but-hasty reader could believe from the passage:
- **true of PART of the passage** but not the whole (a main-idea option that fits paragraph 2 only);
- **true of a person's belief, but not the author's or narrator's conclusion** (the uncle is sure
  the bridge failed from flooding; the passage, read closely, shows otherwise without announcing it);
- **an overgeneralisation of a stated detail** (the passage says the plan worked *in the dry months*;
  the option says it worked);
- **a plausible inference one step too far** (the passage supports "she doubted the survey", the
  option says "she rejected the survey");
- for a paired unit: **the other passage's claim**, or a relation that holds for one passage only.

The passage settles each question through what it shows: sequence, consequence, who turns out to
be right, what the narrator does at the end, how the author qualifies a claim. **Do not dismiss
wrong options outright.** At most ONE explicit rejection in any paragraph, and at most TWO
negation words per paragraph (not, no, never, nothing, nor, none, neither, n't, nobody, nowhere).
No runs of denials, no "it was not X, nor Y". No lists of candidates ("Five explanations were
offered: ...").

### C. Question mix (nine questions), with the ACT reporting category in brackets

- exactly **1 vocabulary-in-context** [Craft and Structure]
- exactly **1 attitude** — the author's or narrator's attitude, tone or point of view [Craft and Structure]
- exactly **1 purpose** — the function of a paragraph, detail or quotation, or how the passage is
  structured [Craft and Structure]
- **2 to 4 inference**, at most **1 detail** ("According to the passage"), at most **1 main-idea**,
  making exactly **4** Key Ideas and Details questions in all
- exactly **2** Integration of Knowledge and Ideas questions: `argument` (how the author supports
  a claim, which claim a piece of evidence supports, what the author would most likely say about a
  new case) or, for a PAIRED unit, `comparison` (how the two passages relate, a point both authors
  would accept, how one author would respond to the other). A paired unit has at least 1
  comparison question; an unpaired unit has none.

### D. Vary what KIND of answer is correct, question to question

Across your nine questions, the correct answer should not always be "what actually happened". Let
some keys be **what a person believed**, some **a plan that was adopted**, some **an implication
the author draws**, some **why something was done**. (Within one question the four options stay
peers: if the key to question 3 is a belief, all four options of question 3 are beliefs.)

### E. Attitude and tone: the reader infers it

The passage must **never name the attitude**: neither the key's attitude word nor any rival's,
nor obvious forms of them (if the options are "admiring", "skeptical", "amused", "regretful",
none of admire/admiring/admiration, skeptic/skeptical, amuse/amused/amusement, regret/regretful
appears in any version). Convey the attitude through what the narrator or author says and does.
The four attitudes are of similar intensity toward one object.

## Standing rules

1. **One narrative or argument, with no asides.** Every sentence belongs to the story or argument.
   Nothing exists only to answer a question.
2. **Choices are peers.** All four choices are the same KIND of thing, share one grammatical frame
   and similar length (longest at most 1.6x the shortest, in characters), each makes one claim, and
   none is hedged, compound or a stance-about-a-claim.
3. **Keys must not be guessable from the options alone.** No choice is the "nicest", most moderate,
   most qualified or most sophisticated. Each is equally plausible before reading.
4. **Avoid world knowledge.** Invented names and places; every world equally possible. Nothing a
   reader could settle from general knowledge of history or science.
5. **Vocabulary-in-context:** 'As it is used in paragraph N, the word "X" most nearly means:'
   (for a paired unit: 'As it is used in Passage A, paragraph N, ...'). X appears **exactly once**
   in each version, in that paragraph, in the same form, used in a different sense in each version.
   Four short glosses in the same part of speech.
6. **Stems** are identical across versions and must make sense with every version. **Cite
   paragraphs or quote phrases; never line numbers** (text reflows on a phone). A stem may not
   contain a word that appears in only one choice of a DIFFERENT question. Every version has the
   **same number of paragraphs** (per passage, for a paired unit), so a paragraph reference means
   the same thing in each.
7. Label each question `difficulty`: easy, medium or hard. Aim for mostly medium and hard; no easy.

## Shape

- Unpaired: each version 720-840 words (hard limits 680-880; longest at most 1.2x the shortest),
  5-10 paragraphs separated by "\n\n".
- Paired: each version is "Passage A" (a header line, e.g. `Passage A by Mara Ellison`), its
  text, then "Passage B" (header line), its text; each part 340-430 words (hard 300-470), 2-6
  paragraphs each, paragraphs separated by "\n\n". The header lines are exactly
  `Passage A ...` and `Passage B ...` on their own lines, followed by a single newline.
  The two passages are by two different (invented) writers on one shared subject.
- Prose at the level of a published ACT Reading passage: literary narrative reads like a story or
  memoir excerpt; social science, humanities and natural science read like a magazine feature or
  essay by a named (invented) writer.

## Support for every version

For each question and each version k, give:
- `why`: a span copied **verbatim** from version k that makes choice k correct;
- `kills`: for EACH of the other three choices j, an object
  `{"kind": "refute" | "mention", "quote": "<verbatim from version k>", "reason": "<one line>"}`.
  - The quote must be **where version k discusses choice j**, so it contains at least one of choice
    j's distinguishing words (or a plain form of one).
  - `refute`: the quote shows choice j is false here. `mention`: the passage discusses choice j but
    does not support it as the answer (a belief that turns out wrong, a detail true of only part,
    a claim one step beyond what is shown). The reason says why that is not enough.
  - The same quote may serve several kills.

## File format (`*.wv.json`)

```json
{
  "passage_id": "AWV1-P01",
  "genre": "social_science",
  "paired": false,
  "passage_title": "short title",
  "versions": [ {"text": "full passage v0 ..."}, {"text": "v1"}, {"text": "v2"}, {"text": "v3"} ],
  "questions": [
    {
      "qid": "AWV1-P01-1", "kind": "inference", "difficulty": "medium",
      "prompt": "It can most reasonably be inferred from the passage that ...",
      "choices": ["c0", "c1", "c2", "c3"],
      "support": [
        { "why": "verbatim from v0",
          "kills": { "1": {"kind": "mention", "quote": "...", "reason": "..."},
                     "2": {"kind": "refute", "quote": "...", "reason": "..."},
                     "3": {"kind": "mention", "quote": "...", "reason": "..."} } },
        "... support for v1 (keys c1; kills 0,2,3) ... through v3 ..."
      ]
    }
  ]
}
```

`genre` is one of `literary_narrative`, `social_science`, `humanities`, `natural_science`.
`kind` is one of: `main-idea`, `detail`, `inference`, `vocabulary-in-context`, `attitude`,
`purpose`, `argument`, `comparison`. qids are `<passage_id>-1` ... `-9`, in the order students see
them (ACT orders roughly by passage position, broad questions first or last).

## Self-check

Count words per version (`wc -w` is fine), check every `why` and `quote` is copied exactly, and
check rule A by hand: for every non-vocabulary, non-attitude question, read each version and
confirm all four options' distinguishing words appear in it. Then reread every version start to
finish, as a reader who has never seen the other three. Each should read like a real published
passage, and in each, exactly one choice per question should survive careful reading, while the
other three should each tempt a reader who skims.

Do not open any file in `scripts/study-bank/` or run any script there. The orchestrator runs the
mechanical check and will send you any problems it finds.
