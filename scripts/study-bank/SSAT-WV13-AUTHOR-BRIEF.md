# SSAT Reading, whole-passage variants: author brief (batch WV13)

You are writing ONE SSAT (Upper Level) reading unit: **five complete versions of one passage** and
**one fixed set of six five-choice questions**. With this method **nobody, including you, knows which
version students will read.**

## The method

- You write five complete passages (v0..v4) on the same topic, in the same genre, voice and length.
  They share the same people, setting and general arc, and differ in what actually happened, what was
  believed, what was decided, why, and how the writer feels about it.
- You write one set of six questions. Each has five choices (c0..c4), and the same stems and choices
  are used with every version.
- **In version k, choice k is the correct answer to every question.** v0 makes c0 correct for all six
  questions, v1 makes c1 correct for all six, and so on. So each version is one coherent "world" in
  which the six c_k answers are all true together. Design the five worlds first, and only then write the
  prose.
- After all units are frozen, a hash-seeded draw picks ONE version per unit, and that is what students
  read. **No version is "the real one."** All five must be equally natural, complete and well written.
  A weak version has a 1-in-5 chance of being the one shown.

## What a good wrong answer looks like (from how real reading tests build them)

Studies of national reading-test items show a clear pattern. The wrong answers that real students
actually choose are **built from things the passage really says, placed wrongly**. The wrong answers
nobody chooses are "plausible-sounding, but the passage never mentions it". Those are filler, and they
make an item easy.

So, for every question, think of the four wrong choices **in the version being read** as lures. Each
lure must be one of these kinds, and each question must use **at least two different kinds**:

| kind | what it is |
|---|---|
| **Stops short** | restates what literally happens on the surface, but stops before the inference the question asks for |
| **Reversed** | reverses a cause and its effect, or the order of two events the passage really contains |
| **Half right** | one element is right and one is wrong (the right person, the wrong reason) |
| **Detail as whole** | a real, vivid detail or one paragraph's topic, offered as the main idea or the purpose |
| **Misplaced detail** | a real detail from the passage attached to the wrong person, thing, moment or relation |
| **Character, not author** | what a character believes or says, where the question asks what the author or narrator concludes |

And these rules for every wrong choice:

1. **It is wrong by one checkable fact in the version being read**: the wrong person, the wrong moment,
   the wrong relation, the wrong scope, or one element false. A careful reader can point to where the
   passage shows it is wrong.
2. **It is never "arguably also right."** An earlier round failed here. When a rival was present but
   left open (the passage said the board "had little money to spend", and a choice said the board acted
   to save money), careful readers defended it as a second answer. The cure is not to deny the rival
   outright. Make it **checkably** wrong: the money worry belongs to a different year, a different
   person, or a different decision than the one asked about.
3. **No flat denials.** If one passage sentence negates the choice's own words ("It was not the
   flood"), the item is too easy. At most one negation word per paragraph (not, no, never, nothing,
   nor, none, neither, n't, nobody, nowhere), and no runs or lists of rejected possibilities.
4. **No filler.** Every choice of every question, apart from vocabulary and attitude, must concern
   people, things or events **present in every version**, in the choice's own distinguishing words (or
   plain forms of them). A check confirms that a reader can never strike a choice because "the passage
   never mentions that", and a person reviews each flag it raises.

## Question set (exactly one of each)

`main-idea`, `detail`, `inference`, `vocabulary-in-context`, `attitude`, `purpose`.

- **Main idea:** at least one wrong choice is **as broad as the key**, either an over-generalisation or a
  broad statement of the wrong focus, so that "pick the broadest option" never decides the item.
  Typical lures: detail-as-whole, and one paragraph's topic offered as the whole.
- **Detail** ("According to the passage"): the lures are misplaced details. They take the words right
  next to the answer, or a real fact from elsewhere attached to the wrong thing.
- **Inference:** the lures are stops-short (the literal surface event), one-step-too-far, and
  character-not-author.
- **Purpose** (of a paragraph or a detail): lures are detail-as-whole and reversed function. The item
  must not be settled by the paragraph's topic alone: the five purposes should all be things that
  paragraph COULD be doing.
- **Vocabulary-in-context** (SAT words-in-context style, STRICTER than before):
  - Stem: use **your unit's assigned vocabulary phrasing** from the table in "Your unit" below (a
    checker enforces it). X appears in the same form, in that paragraph, in all five versions. In version k exactly one sense fits X's sentence:
    sense k.
  - **Choose X from YOUR unit's headword list**, which the orchestrator gives you with your unit id. A
    checker refuses any other word. Every word on your list has at least four clearly separate senses of
    the kind a learner's dictionary lists as separate numbered entries. Use the word in the listed form.
  - **The five senses must be UNRELATED meanings of X, overlapping in NO context.** A checker gives two
    readers every pair of your five glosses and asks whether any ordinary sentence could support both.
    If both readers find such a sentence for any pair, the unit is refused.
    - Take the five senses from separate dictionary entries, not from shades of one entry. Two senses
      fail when one is a figurative use of the other, when one is a kind of the other, or when one
      ordinary phrase could carry both.
    - Before you keep a gloss, try to write one ordinary sentence in which X could carry it AND another of
      your glosses. If you can, replace one of them.
    - Five short glosses in one frame and the same part of speech. Each fits the grammar of X's
      sentence in its own version.
  - **Each version keys its OWN gloss.** In version k, X's sentence (in the stem's paragraph) admits sense
    k and only sense k, and that sentence is version k's `why` for the vocabulary question. The checker
    confirms this after every change, and two readers check each version.
  - No transparent words and no rare dictionary senses.
- **Attitude.** WV13 has TWO attitude forms (almost every unit uses Form A). Your unit's form is fixed in "Your unit" below. In both forms:
  - **Five FELT attitudes from five different directions**, one word from each class. A checker
    refuses any other word, two from one class, and any "no feeling" word. **Irony words are refused**
    (ironic, mocking, wry, sarcastic, sardonic): the amused direction is plain enjoyment only.

    | direction | allowed words |
    |---|---|
    | warm | admiring, approving, appreciative, proud, respectful, grateful, sympathetic, fond, affectionate, enthusiastic, reverent, tender |
    | critical | critical, disapproving, scornful, contemptuous, indignant, irritated, resentful, exasperated, disdainful, annoyed, angry |
    | anxious | uneasy, worried, apprehensive, anxious, wary, fearful, alarmed, troubled |
    | sorrowful | regretful, sad, wistful, nostalgic, mournful, melancholy, rueful, sorrowful |
    | amused | amused, playful, humorous, whimsical |

    (Noun forms such as "admiration", "regret", "worry" or "amusement" also count, and are what a
    "conveys a feeling of" stem needs.)
  - **The attitude must be INFERABLE, never announced.** No version may contain ANY word from the
    table above (or ironic, mocking, wry), in any form or about anyone, nor tone-announcing words such
    as comic, funny, laughable, amusing, absurd, admirable, shameful, regrettable, sadly, worrying,
    troubling or delightful. A checker refuses them.
  - **Everything is read LITERALLY.** No irony, dryness, understatement, sarcasm or teasing in any
    sentence that carries the attitude. Earlier rounds failed when a dry remark read as a jab as well as
    plain irritation, and when a joke read as criticism.
  - **The licensing checker.** For every version, two readers who do not know the key must find exactly
    one defensible attitude, and must quote a sentence that excludes each of the other four.

### Form A: the AUTHOR's or NARRATOR's attitude toward the subject (`"attitude_form": "author"`)

For an essay, a memoir or a nature piece. The question asks how the WRITER (the essayist, the memoir's
narrator, the nature writer) feels about the SUBJECT of the passage, which the stem names ("the new
signs", "the hives", "the swifts' roost"). It is never about a character's reaction.

- **Stem:** your unit's assigned phrasing from "Your unit" below, filled in naturally (a checker enforces
  the frame). The subject named in the stem is the same in all five versions.
- **The writer's attitude toward that subject differs between versions,** and in version k it is
  exactly the attitude of choice k.
- **Licensed by at least TWO SEPARATE SENTENCES in every version.** Two different sentences of the
  version, usually in different paragraphs (for example one in the body and one at the close), each
  show the same attitude on its own. Declare them as the attitude question's `why` and `why2`; each is
  a FULL sentence quoted from its start to its end (at least six words), copied exactly, and they are two
  different sentences. A checker refuses a missing, fragmentary, non-verbatim or shared sentence, and
  the two licensing readers must each also find two such sentences themselves.
- **One direction, through the whole version.** No sentence anywhere in a version points to another
  direction. Earlier rounds failed on exactly these neighbouring pairs, and these are the rules that
  separate them:
  - **warm** credits the subject: the writer praises what it is or does, or honours the people behind
    it. Nothing about it is ending or lost, and nothing threatens it.
  - **critical** objects to the subject plainly: the writer says what is wrong with it and what should
    have been done instead. No joke, no regret for what is gone.
  - **anxious** looks FORWARD: the writer fears a harm to the subject that has not happened yet and may
    not be avoidable. Nothing is lost yet; nobody is blamed.
  - **sorrowful** looks BACK: the writer dwells on something about the subject that is already gone and
    cannot return. No future threat, no blame, no praise of what replaced it.
  - **amused** enjoys the subject: the writer delights in something harmless and comic about it (a
    coincidence, a happy mix-up, an odd habit) where nobody is hurt, blamed or mocked.

  A loss that is also a tribute (wistful vs admiring), a joke that is also a complaint (amused vs
  critical), and an ending that looks both back and ahead (wistful vs worried) each license two answers.
  **Give each version's two licensing sentences a context that rules the rival reading out.**
- **Declare `"narrator_role"`**: "participant" for a memoir, "observer" for an essay or nature piece
  whose writer is not part of the events.

### Form B: a CHARACTER's feeling shown by an action (`"attitude_form": "character"`)

For narrative fiction (in this batch, diary fiction). The character-action form used in earlier batches, unchanged:

- **Stem shape:** your unit's assigned phrasing from "Your unit" below (a checker enforces it). It names
  the character, the action or reply, and the paragraph, and is never about the author, the narrator or a
  tone. The stem is identical in all five versions, so that action or reply exists in every version, in
  the same paragraph number. **What the character actually does or says differs between versions,** and
  in version k it licenses exactly the feeling of choice k.
- **The action or reply is at least ONE FULL SENTENCE in every version** (a checker refuses shorter):
  something the character does or says that is a whole sentence of six or more words. Never a two-word
  reply like "Ready, yes." Quote that whole sentence as the version's `why`.
- **Every version's action is read LITERALLY.** The amused version shows the character plainly enjoying
  something: laughing, playing, delighting in a coincidence. It never teases another person. The critical
  version states its objection directly.
- **The answer must be inferable from that action in its context.** The reader looks at what the
  character does or says, and at what led up to it, and infers the feeling. No sentence may name it.
- **Make the five actions point five different ways, unmistakably.** A warm action credits or helps
  someone. A critical action rebukes or refuses someone. An anxious action guards against a danger that
  is still to come. A sorrowful action dwells on something already lost. An amused action plays or
  enjoys. Give each version's action a context that rules the rival reading out: a joke made to someone
  the character plainly likes, about something that cost nothing, cannot be a jab.
- **Declare `"narrator_role"`** as "observer" or "participant".

a fill-in of the pattern. Write `<N>` as a digit ("paragraph 4"); the paragraph must be the same in all

## The five worlds must be equally likely from the options alone

A checker shows each unit's six questions together, without any passage, to readers who try to
assemble the most coherent story from the options. If one world is the "obvious story" (its main
idea, inference and purpose options fit together better than the others do), the unit is refused
before the draw. Make all five worlds equally coherent and equally surprising. Do not let a single
detail (a flood, a rival, a date) point to one version's answers across several questions.

## Vary what KIND of answer is correct

Across the six questions, the correct answer should not always be "what actually happened". Let some
keys be what a character believed, some a plan that was adopted, some an implication the author draws,
some why something was done. Within one question the five choices stay peers: if question 3's key is a
belief, all five of its choices are beliefs.

## Standing rules

1. **One narrative or argument, no asides.** Every sentence belongs to the story or argument, and nothing
   exists only to answer a question. Earlier rounds were marked down for a "loosely connected filler"
   paragraph.
2. **Choices are peers.** All five choices are the same KIND of thing and share one grammatical frame.
   They are of similar length: the longest is **at most 1.5x the shortest**, in characters. Each makes
   one claim, and none is hedged, compound, or a stance about a claim.
3. **No choice is the "nicest", most moderate, or most sophisticated.** Each is equally plausible before
   the passage is read.
4. **Avoid world knowledge.** Use invented names and places, and keep every world equally possible.
5. **Stems** are identical across versions and must make sense with every version. A stem may not contain
   a word that appears in only one choice of a DIFFERENT question. If a stem names a paragraph number,
   the paragraph counts must line up across versions.
6. Label each question's `difficulty` as easy, medium or hard. Aim for medium and hard. Hardness should
   come from one strong lure built from real passage content placed wrongly, **not** from a long chain of
   inference.
7. Prose at the level of a published SSAT Upper Level passage: natural, specific, with a real voice. Never
   write the template rhythm of "Four explanations were offered: ... The records settle it: ...".

## Shape

- Each version is 300-360 words (hard limits 270-380; the longest version at most 1.35x the shortest),
  in 4-5 paragraphs separated by "\n\n" (hard limits 3-6).

## Support for every version

For each question and each version k, give:
- `why`: a span copied **verbatim** from version k that makes choice k correct;
- `kills`: for EACH of the other four choices j, an object
  `{"kind": "refute" | "mention", "lure": "<one of: stops-short, reversed, half-right, detail-as-whole, misplaced-detail, character-not-author>", "quote": "<verbatim from version k>", "reason": "<one line: the one checkable fact that makes it wrong here>"}`.
  - The quote must be **where version k discusses choice j**, so it contains at least one of choice j's
    distinguishing words (or a plain form of one).
  - The same quote may serve several kills.
- **Author-form attitude question only:** each version's support ALSO has `why2`, the second full
  licensing sentence (see Form A). `why` and `why2` are two different whole sentences.

## File format (`*.wv.json`)

```json
{
  "passage_id": "WV13-P01",
  "genre": "...",
  "narrator_role": "observer",
  "attitude_form": "author",
  "versions": [ {"text": "full passage v0 ..."}, {"text": "v1"}, {"text": "v2"}, {"text": "v3"}, {"text": "v4"} ],
  "questions": [
    {
      "qid": "WV13-P01-1", "kind": "main-idea", "difficulty": "medium",
      "prompt": "The passage is primarily concerned with",
      "choices": ["c0", "c1", "c2", "c3", "c4"],
      "support": [
        { "why": "verbatim from v0",
          "kills": { "1": {"kind": "mention", "lure": "detail-as-whole", "quote": "...", "reason": "..."},
                     "2": {...}, "3": {...}, "4": {...} } },
        "... support for v1 (keys c1; kills 0,2,3,4) ... through v4 ..."
      ]
    }
  ]
}
```

`kind` is one of: `main-idea`, `detail`, `inference`, `vocabulary-in-context`, `attitude`, `purpose`.
The qids are `<passage_id>-1` through `-6`, in the order students see them.

## Self-check

- Count the words in each version (`wc -w` is fine).
- Check that every `why`, `why2` and `quote` is copied exactly.
- Author form: in every version, find your two licensing sentences and check that neither one could
  also be read as a neighbouring direction (admiring/wistful, amused/critical, worried/wistful).
- Check rule 4 by hand: in every version, every non-vocabulary, non-attitude choice's distinguishing words
  appear.
- Reread every version start to finish as a reader who has never seen the other four. Each should read
  like a real published passage. In each version, exactly one choice per question should survive careful
  reading, while each of the other four should tempt a reader who skims, and be wrong by one fact the
  reader can find.

Do not open any file in `scripts/study-bank/` or run any script there. The orchestrator runs the
mechanical check and will send you any problems it finds.
