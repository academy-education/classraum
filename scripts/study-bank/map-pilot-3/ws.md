## Passage for items 17-21

Strand: Literary Text | Grade target: 6 | Target band: RIT 190-199

I had promised Ms. Reyes four dozen oatmeal cookies for our class bake sale on Saturday, so bringing them was my job. Grandma Bea left her recipe card on the counter and asked if I wanted help, but I told her I could do the work myself.

I had set myself a goal of forty-eight cookies, every one of them perfect. The card said to use softened butter, but the butter was still hard from the refrigerator, so to save time I melted it in the microwave, which seemed like a clever shortcut. The first tray came out flat, the cookies spread wide and touching. It was a mistake, and an embarrassing one.

Grandma watched from the doorway. A few minutes later Ana came over with the sign she had painted for our table. 'You can still save it,' she said. 'Break it into pieces and call them oat crisps. They might sell even better.' I frowned at the ruined tray. 'I promised cookies,' I said. 'Not crumbs in a bag.'

I tried to fix what went wrong by baking the rest of the batch in small, careful spoonfuls, but only twenty came out round. I boxed those and pushed the flat ones toward the trash. When I looked up, Grandma had stuck a strip of tape on the big tin by the front door that said Ms. Reyes, 4 dozen. I stood there a long time, then I packed every cookie, flat ones too.

On Saturday two families had dropped out of the sale, and our table looked half empty until I opened the tin. Ms. Reyes counted. 'Forty-eight. That is exactly what we needed.' The buyers took the flat ones as quickly as the round ones.

## Passage for items 30-34

Strand: Informational Text | Grade target: 7 | Target band: RIT 200-209

For forty years, the only way across the Marrow River at Pellam Ford was a wooden raft pulled along a steel cable. Children from the west bank rode it to school, and the ferry was how Otto Brandt, the ferryman, made his living.

In the fall of 1956, at a town council meeting, a teacher named Ida Venn raised the idea of a footbridge. Most people in town had ridden with Otto since childhood and saw the bridge as a threat to the ferryman's living.

That winter Ida counted crossings at the landing and found more than three hundred a week, far more than a few families. Her students began fundraising and soon had enough money for the railings. The count showed the bridge would be no waste, yet members kept putting off a vote; Otto's feelings worried them more than the cost. Otto had run the ferry safely in high water for forty years.

In April, high water reached the landing steps, and Otto tried one crossing with three children aboard. Halfway over, the cable slipped, and the raft swung toward the rapids before it caught. Everyone reached shore, shaking. That night Otto told his wife that the ferry had become too risky to run in high water, and that he had changed his mind about the bridge.

The council, which had seen high water every few springs, made no change after the flood. Then, at the May meeting, Otto stood up and gave the bridge his support. He told them that his living mattered less than the children's safety. Members trusted the man who knew the river best and approved the bridge that night. It opened in October 1957, and Otto walked across it first.
