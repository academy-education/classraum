PASSAGE P1: The Fern in the Jar
=====================================================

[1] In 1829 a London doctor named Nathaniel Ward buried the chrysalis of a moth in damp soil at the bottom of a glass jar, and sealed the lid. He wanted to watch the adult insect emerge. The moth did hatch, but by then Ward had noticed something more interesting: a small fern and a few blades of grass had sprouted from the soil.

[2] Ward had tried for years to grow ferns in his garden, and every attempt had failed. The air in his part of London was thick with coal smoke, which choked delicate plants. Inside the jar, however, the fern thrived. Water evaporated from the soil, condensed on the glass, and trickles back down, so the plant never needed watering. The seal also kept the smoke out. Ward left the jar on a windowsill for nearly four years without opening it, and the fern stayed green the entire time.

[3] [A] Ward soon had a carpenter build larger versions: wooden boxes with glass walls and tight lids. [B] He then wondered whether the cases could protect plants on long sea voyages. [C] In 1833 he packed two cases with ferns and grasses and shipped them to Sydney, where nearly every plant arrived alive after months on deck. [D] The return trip went just as well.

[4] The invention reshaped the plant trade. Before Wards' cases, nurseries that ordered plants from abroad had expected to lose nearly all of them; afterward, most survived. Around 1850 the botanist Robert Fortune used the cases to carry thousands of tea plants from China to India, there they became the start of a vast tea industry. Rubber and banana plants later traveled the same way.

[5] At home, the cases had a gentler effect. Victorian families set glass cases of ferns in their parlors, and a craze of collecting ferns swept the country. Ward himself kept a case on his desk for the rest of his life.

PASSAGE P2: The Mold That Drew a Railway
=====================================================

[1] Physarum polycephalum has no brain, no nerves, and no eyes. It is a slime mold, a single enormous cell that creeps across the forest floor as a bright yellow film, feeding on bacteria and fungal spores. Yet in the past few decades this organism has solved problems that would challenge a team of engineers.

[2] In 2000 the Japanese researcher Toshiyuki Nakagaki placed pieces of a slime mold throughout a small plastic maze. The pieces merged into a single cell who filled every corridor. He then set an oat flake, one of the mold's favorite foods at the entrance and another at the exit. Within hours, for example, the mold had withdrawn from the dead ends, leaving one thick tube along the shortest route between the two flakes.

[3] Ten years later, Nakagaki and his colleague Atsushi Tero tried something more ambitious. On a wet dish shaped like the region around Tokyo, they placed oat flakes in positions, matching thirty-six cities and towns. The mold started at the spot representing Tokyo itself. Because it avoids bright light, the researchers lit the areas that stood for mountains and lakes, which kept the mold off ground where rail lines would be hard to build. After about a day the mold had linked every flake with a web of tubes. When the team compared that web with the real rail network around Tokyo, they found the two remarkably alike in their efficiency and in how well they would survive a broken link.

[4] The mold's method is simple. It begins by spreading in all directions. Tubes that carry a strong flow of nutrients between food sources thickens; tubes that carry little thin out and eventually vanish. No part of the cell plans the network, it emerges from countless local adjustments.

[5] Engineers have taken note. Researchers have since written computer programs that copy the mold's rules, and they hope such programs could help design road systems, power grids, and communication networks that keep working when one connection fails.

=====================================================

[1] The summer I turned sixteen, my cousin Ifeoma found me a job at Benedek Press, a print shop two blocks from our apartment. I pictured myself running a copier and answering the phone. PASSAGE P4: The Otter and the Forest
=====================================================

[1] Off the coast of Alaska's Aleutian Islands, two kinds of seafloor lie within a short boat ride of each other. Around some islands, forests of kelp rises from the rocks, their golden fronds reaching the surface and sheltering schools of fish. Around others, the bottom is nearly bare, covered with spiny purple sea urchins and little else.

[2] In 1971 a young ecologist named James Estes set out to learn why. He noticed that the islands with kelp forests had large numbers of sea otters, while the bare islands had almost none. Otters eat sea urchins, and sea urchins eat kelp. Where otters were plentiful, he reasoned they kept the urchins in check, and the kelp was free to grow. Where otters were absent, for example, urchins multiplied and grazed the forests down to the rock.

[3] The otters' absence from some islands had a human cause. For more than a century, fur traders had hunted the animals for their pelts, that carry the densest fur of any mammal, about a million hairs in a single square inch. By 1911, when an international treaty finally banned the hunt, only a few small colonies survived. Some islands had been recolonized by the time Estes arrived; others had not.

[4] Estes's idea (an animal at the top of a food chain can shape the plants at it's bottom) is now called a trophic cascade. In the 1990s, it received a routine test. Otter numbers across the Aleutians suddenly crashed, and within a few years urchins had stripped many forests bare. Estes and his colleagues suspected killer whales, which may have begun hunting otters after their usual prey grew scarce.

[5] The story shows how much can depend on a single species. An otter weighs about as much as a large dog, but by eating urchins it protects an entire forest and every creature that lives there.
