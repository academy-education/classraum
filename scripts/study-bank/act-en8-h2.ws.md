PASSAGE P7: Footsteps on Demand
=====================================================

[1] When a character in a film walks across a gravel driveway, the crunch the audience hears was almost never recorded on that driveway. Microphones on a set are aimed at the dialogue, they pick up footsteps, rustling coats, and clinking cups only faintly. Those sounds are added afterward, in a studio, by specialists called Foley artists.

[2] The craft takes its name from Jack Foley, a Universal Studios employee who begins adding sounds to films in the late 1920s, when movies first acquired soundtracks. His method, which is still the basis of the work, was simple: watch the picture on a screen and perform the sounds in time with it.

[3] A Foley studio looks less like a recording booth than a cluttered garage. Its floor is divided into shallow pits of sand, gravel, wood, and tile, and the shelves along its back wall holds shoes, gloves, keys, and boxes of objects chosen for how they sound rather than how they look. A bunch of celery, snapped close to the microphone, qualifies as a broken bone. Cornstarch squeezed inside a leather pouch makes the squeak of boots in fresh snow.

[4] What matters most, though, is timing. An artist walking in place on a gravel pit must set each foot down at the instant the actor on screen does and must match the weight and mood of the performance as well. A tired soldier and a nervous thief do not put their feet down the same way. In fact, experienced Foley artists often say that they act with their feet.

[5] Digital libraries now hold millions of recorded sounds. An editor can drop a stock footstep into a scene in seconds. Studios also hire Foley artists. A library footstep is identical every time it plays, whereas a performed one carries the small irregularities of a real persons stride, and audiences hear the difference even when they cannot name it.

PASSAGE P9: Tomorrow by Telegraph
=====================================================

[1] Until the middle of the nineteenth century, nobody could tell a ship's captain what the weather would do tomorrow. Sailors read the clouds and their barometers, but a storm moving in from the Atlantic usually arrived faster than any news of it could. [2] That office belonged to Robert FitzRoy, a naval officer who had went around the world as captain of HMS Beagle, the ship that carried the young Charles Darwin. A storm in 1859 that sank the passenger ship Royal Charter persuaded him of one thing, the data could be used to warn of storms before they arrived. By 1861 his office was sending storm warnings by telegraph to ports, where signals were hoisted for ships in harbor to see.

[3] Later that year FitzRoy went further. [A] Beginning in August 1861, The Times of London printed his forecasts for the general public. [B] Each forecast gave the expected wind and weather for several regions of Britain over the following two days. [C] FitzRoy himself chose the word forecast for these notices, explaining that they were informed judgments rather than prophecies. [D]

[4] The forecasts were often wrong, and the number of letters complaining about the errors was growing. Some scientists argued that the forecasts had no sound theory behind it and that a government office should not publish guesses. FitzRoy, who had defended the work in print, was by then in failing health, and he died in 1865. The next year, after a review judged his methods unreliable, the public forecasts were stopped.

[5] Therefore, the storm warnings were back within two years, restored after complaints from ports and fishing towns, and daily forecasts returned to the newspapers more than a decade later. Today's forecasts rest on satellites and computer models that FitzRoy could not have imagined, but they still begin where his did, with many observations gathered quickly in one place.

PASSAGE P10: What the Basket Holds
=====================================================

[1] When a news report announces that prices rose three percent last year, the figure almost always comes from a consumer price index. The idea behind such an index is simple enough to explain at a kitchen table. Picture a shopping basket that holds what a typical household buys in a month: bread, bus fares, rent, a haircut, a small share of a winter coat. Price the same basket a year later, and the change in its total cost shows how much more it takes to live the same way.

[2] Filling the basket is the hard part. In the United States, the Bureau of Labor Statistics decides what belongs in it by surveying thousands of households about what it spends. The weight given to each category depend on how much of the average budget it absorbs. Housing, which takes the largest share of most budgets, counts for far more than postage stamps. Each month, data collectors note the prices of tens of thousands of specific items at stores, clinics, and websites across dozens of cities. A collector who priced one brand of canned soup in March will look for the same can in April, the point is to compare like with like.

[3] The basket also has to keep up with the way people live. Some goods improve faster than their prices change. Statisticians therefore estimate how much of a price difference reflects better quality rather than inflation. Shoppers change, too. However, when the price of beef climbs, many shoppers buy more chicken, and an index that kept the old basket fixed would report a larger rise then the one most households actually felt.

[4] No real family buys exactly that basket. A renter who bikes to work and a homeowner with a long commute live through very different years, even when the headline number is the same for both. That is why economists treat the index as a careful average rather than a portrait of anyone's grocery bill, and why a particular household's prices can feel totally out of whack with the figure on the evening news.

PASSAGE P11: The Air Beneath the Ice
=====================================================

[1] Snow that falls on the high interior of Antarctica does not go anywhere. In places where summers never warm enough to melt it. Each winter's snow is buried by the next, and the weight of later layers slowly presses the older ones into solid ice. As the grains fuse, small pockets of air between them are sealed off.

[2] To reach that air, a drilling team bores down through the ice sheet and lifts out a core: a cylinder of ice about as wide as a coffee can, brought up in sections a few meters long. Stored at temperatures far below freezing, scientists can keep the cores for decades. Back in the laboratory, researchers crush small pieces of ice in a vacuum and look at the gases that escape. The deepest cores from Antarctica reaches back roughly 800,000 years, and the cores' oldest layers hold air that has been sealed away ever since.

[3] What that air shows is striking. During the coldest stretches of the ice ages, carbon dioxide stood at around 180 parts per million; in the warm periods between them, it rose to roughly 280. The ice carries a second record as well. The ratio of heavy to light oxygen atoms in frozen water depends on how cold the air was when the snow formed. Nonetheless, each layer tells researchers how cold it was as well as what the air held. When scientists first lined up the two records in the late 1980s, they find that carbon dioxide and temperature had risen and fallen together.

[4] Today the level is above 400 parts per million, higher than at any point in the ice's record. Against a history measured in hundreds of thousands of years, the speed of the recent change is what stands out most. The cores cannot say what comes next, but they can show what the planet was like each time its air changed before.
