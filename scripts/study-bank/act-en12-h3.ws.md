PASSAGE P8: Keep It Turning
=====================================================

[1] I signed up for the glassblowing class because the brochure showed a row of glowing vases and I doubted I would make one by the end of the first night. Esperanza Ruelas, who ran the studio out of an old garage on the edge of town had other plans. She handed each of us a steel pipe as long as a broom handle and told us we would spend the evening learning to turn it.

[2] Turning, it turned out, was everything. Molten glass is about as thick as honey, and the moment you stop rotating the pipe, gravity starts pulling the gather toward the floor. [A] For two hours we roll empty pipes back and forth along the arms of a metal bench. [B] My wrists ached by nine o'clock. [C] Esperanza walked behind us, saying nothing except, now and then, "Slower." [D] By the end I could keep a pipe spinning without thinking about it.

[3] The second week we finally worked with real glass. Esperanza dipped the end of my pipe into the furnace and drew out a glowing ball no bigger than a plum. I rolled it on a steel table to shape it, reheated it, and blew a short puff of air into the pipe. The ball shrank slightly and wobbled. Then it slid off the end onto the concrete floor, where it laid glowing like a dropped coal.

[4] I expected her to be annoyed. Instead, she knelt, looked at the blob for a moment, and said that I had stopped turning to watch the bubble grow. She was right, I had been so pleased with myself that I had forgotten the one thing I had practiced for two hours.

[5] By the end of the course I had made six lopsided cups and one small bowl that almost sat flat. The bowl holds paper clips on my desk now. Whenever I look at it, I can still feel the weight of the pipe turning in my hands.

PASSAGE P9: Let Them Sleep
=====================================================

[1] Every school morning, thousands of teenagers in our district stand at bus stops in the dark, many of them half asleep. The first bell at our high school rings at 7:25, which means many students wake before six. Most of them are not getting enough sleep, and the reason is not, as adults like to assume that they stay up too late on their phones.

[2] Around puberty, the body's internal clock shifts. The hormone that makes people drowsy begin to be released about two hours later in the evening than it was in childhood, so a typical sixteen-year-old cannot easily fall asleep before eleven. Teenagers need between eight and ten hours of sleep a night. A student who falls asleep at eleven and wakes at six gets seven at most. No amount of discipline at bedtime can close that gap.

[3] When schools have moved their start times later, the results have been striking. However, in 2016 Seattle pushed its high schools' first bell from 7:50 to 8:45. Researchers whom tracked students there found that they slept about half an hour longer on school nights, and their grades in one biology course rose. Attendance improved as well. The American Academy of Pediatrics has recommended since 2014 that middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30.

[4] Critics raise absurd concerns. Buses would have to be rescheduled, and practices for sports teams would run later into the evening. Both changes would cost money and require planning. These problems are real, but other districts have solved them, often by flipping the schedules so that elementary schools, whose younger students wake early anyway, start first.

[5] The evidence is clear, and every year of delay costs another class of students its sleep. Our school board will vote on a new schedule next month, it should approve an 8:30 start for the high school.

PASSAGE P10: Twelve Pixels Square
=====================================================

[1] In 1999 a Japanese mobile phone company asked a young employee named Shigetaka Kurita to solve a problem. The company's new service let customers send short e-mails from their phones but each message was limited to 250 characters. Kurita worried that such brief notes would be easy to forge. A plain "Fine" could sound cheerful or cold, and the screen offered no way to tell which.

[2] His solution was a set of 176 tiny pictures; each drawn on a grid only twelve pixels wide and twelve pixels tall. He took ideas from weather forecasts, which used a sun or a cloud to sum up a day, from the symbols in comic books, and he also borrowed from the Chinese characters used in written Japanese. He called them emoji, from the Japanese words for "picture" and "character." Despite the resemblance, the word has nothing to do with the English word "emotion."

[3] The pictures were an instant hit, and rival companies in Japan soon created their own sets. That success, for instance, caused a new problem. A heart sent from one company's phone might arrive on another's as a blank box or a string of nonsense, because each company coded their pictures differently.

[4] The fix came from the Unicode Consortium, a group that assigns a standard number to every letter and symbol in the world's writing systems so that a letter typed on one computer appears correctly on all the others. In 2010, after several years of debate, the consortium added hundreds of emoji to its standard. Within a few years, phones around the world included an emoji keyboard.

[5] In 2016 the Museum of Modern Art in New York acquired Kurita's original 176 designs for its permanent collection. Next to today's detailed, full-color emoji, they look crude, but every one of the thousands that followed descend from them.
