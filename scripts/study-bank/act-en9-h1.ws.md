PASSAGE P3: What the River Carried
=====================================================

[1] In the summer of 1858, the river through London began to smell so strongly that people crossing its bridges held cloths over their faces. The city's household drains and overflowing cesspits had emptied into the Thames, and during that unusually hot June the low water left the waste baking in the sun. Newspapers soon gave the episode a name. They called it the Great Stink.

[2] The smell reached even the Houses of Parliament, which stood on the riverbank at Westminster. Members tried soaking the building's curtains in chemicals to block the odor, and some talked of moving their sessions out of London altogether. Westminster Abbey, which stands nearby, had been the site of English coronations since the eleventh century. In contrast, lawmakers who had put off the problem for years passed a bill giving the Metropolitan Board of Works the money and authority to build new sewers.

[3] The engineer chosen for the job was Joseph Bazalgette, whose plans had been stalled for two years by arguments over cost. His solution was to intercept the waste. He designed large sewers that ran parallel to the river they caught the old drains before those drains could reach the Thames. The sewers then carried the flow east to outfalls, which were located many miles downstream at a considerable distance from the city. He also made the pipes far larger than the city then needed—a choice that let the system keep up with a population that grew well beyond what anyone in 1858 had predicted.

[4] The work required digging tunnels, laying hundreds of millions of bricks, and the construction of pumping stations to lift the waste where the ground was too flat for gravity. Along the north bank, the Victoria Embankment was built partly to hold one of the main sewers. Once the main lines were finished in the 1870s the river through central London no longer received most of the city's sewage. Much of that network still carries London's waste today.

[5] Bazalgette did not know that cholera spreads through contaminated water; like most engineers of his day, he blamed foul air. Yet by carrying sewage away from the crowded center, his system helped end the cholera outbreaks that had struck London several times in earlier decades.

On the second day I spotted a dark smudge of them on the route I had chosen, and we swung half a mile east rather than walk to the edge of a forty-foot drop.

[5] By the last morning I was choosing our route myself, and Tobias was trailing behind with his hands in his pockets. I still carry my phone on every hike, charged and switched off at the bottom of my pack. However, I no longer reach for it first. What the map gave me was not a better way of getting somewhere but a picture of the place I was moving through, and I would not trade that picture for any blue dot.

The gates of each pair meet at an angle that points upstream, so the weight of the water on the higher side presses them shut rather than forcing them open. Set into the gates are paddles—small sliding panels—that let water into or out of the chamber.

[3] [A] To raise a boat, the keeper first lets the chamber drain to the level of the lower canal and opens their lower gates. [B] The boat motors in, and the gates swing closed behind it. [C] Then the paddles in the upper gates were opened, and water from the higher canal pours into the chamber, lifting the boat. [D] When the water inside matches the level outside, the upper gates open and the boat continues on its way.

[4] Lowering a boat simply reverses the order. The boat enters a full chamber from above, the upper gates close behind it, and the keeper raises the lower paddles. The water drains out downward into the lower canal until the two levels match, and the lower gates swing open. Each time a boat passes, then, a full chamber of water flows from the upper canal toward the lower one. Instead, the highest stretch of a canal loses water to leaks and evaporation, so engineers feed it from streams or reservoirs to keep it full.

[5] Because each step uses gravity rather than an engine a lock can keep working for a century with little more than new gates every few decades. Some flights of locks in Britain, built more than two hundred years ago, still lift boats today. Each chamber in such a flight holding back a little more of the hillside. Climbing one takes hours, but no part of the climb needs a pump.
