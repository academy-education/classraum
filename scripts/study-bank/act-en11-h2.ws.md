PASSAGE P5: The Great Stink
=====================================================

[1] In the summer of 1858, London baked under some of the hottest weather anyone could remember, and the River Thames began to stink. For centuries the city had emptied its waste into the river, and by the 1850s, when the city had more than two million residents, the Thames was little more than an open sewer. As the water warmed, the smell grew so powerful that newspapers called it the Great Stink.

[2] The stench reached even the Houses of Parliament, which stand on the riverbank. Workers hung curtains soaked in chemicals over the windows, but lawmakers still fled the committee rooms with handkerchiefs pressed to their faces. Within weeks, Parliament approves a plan it has been putting off for years.

[3] The plan belonged to Joseph Bazalgette, chief engineer of the citys' Metropolitan Board of Works. He proposed a network of large brick sewers running alongside the Thames that would catch the waste before it reached the river and carry it miles to the east, where it could be liberated on the outgoing tide. Bazalgette, whom colleagues knew for his caution, worked out how wide each pipe needed to be and then doubled the figure, reasoning that the job would be done only once.

[4] Most Londoners of the time believed that diseases such as cholera spread through foul air, and Bazalgette's sewers were built to remove a smell. For example, they had an effect that no one had planned. Waste no longer flowed into the river where much of the city drew its drinking water from, and cholera, which had killed tens of thousands of Londoners in earlier outbreaks, largely disappeared. The last major outbreak, in 1866, struck a district that had not yet been connected to the new system.

[5] Much of Bazalgette's network is still in use, in recent years London has built a new tunnel beneath the Thames to handle the overflow from a city far larger than the one he planned for.

PASSAGE P6: Word-Cross
=====================================================

[1] On December 21, 1913, readers of the New York World found something new in the paper's Sunday supplement: a diamond-shaped grid of empty squares with a list of numbered clues beneath it. Its creator, a Liverpool-born journalist named Arthur Wynne, called it a "Word-Cross." Within a few weeks, a typesetting mix-up had reversed the name, and the puzzle has been a crossword ever since.

[2] For a decade, a period of ten years, the World was nearly the only paper to print one. Then, in 1924, a young publishing firm called Simon & Schuster released a book of crosswords; its first title. The book sold hundreds of thousands of copies, and a craze followed. Bands recorded songs about crosswords, and one railroad reportedly placed dictionaries in its passenger cars for puzzling travelers. Dictionaries flew off store shelves, and libraries complained that puzzle solvers were sharing their reference books.

[3] Not everyone was charmed. For example, the New York Times dismissed the crossword as a "sinful waste" of time. The paper eventually changed its mind, in 1942 it began running a Sunday puzzle, edited by a woman named Margaret Farrar. She would hold the job for more than twenty-five years.

[4] Farrar shaped the crossword more than anyone since Wynne. She required symmetrical grids, words of at least three letters, and for clues to be fair as well as clever. Her rules, along with her habit of testing every clue on a second solver, is still followed by most American puzzle editors.

[5] A century after Wynne's diamond, the crossword appears in hundreds of newspapers and on millions of phones. Its basic appeal has changed little since 1913. A good clue misleads without lying: "Bark," for instance, may point to a dog or to a tree. The pleasure of solving lies in the moment when a clue that seemed impossible suddenly makes sense.

PASSAGE P7: The Long Wait
=====================================================

[1] In the spring of certain years, the ground beneath the forests of the eastern United States begins to open. Small holes appear by the thousands, and one warm evening, as if on a signal, millions of insects crawl out of it at once. They are periodical cicadas, and they have spent the last thirteen or seventeen years underground.

[2] For nearly all of that time, a young cicada, called a nymph, lives in the dark, sucking sap from the roots of trees. It grows slowly, molting several times. How the nymphs keep count of the years are still not fully understood; some researchers think they track the yearly rise and fall of sap in the roots. When the time comes, the nymphs wait for the soil about eight inches down to warm to roughly 64 degrees Fahrenheit and then emerge.

[3] [A] Above ground, the adults have only a few weeks to live. [B] The males gather in the treetops and sing, producing a chorus loud enough to drown out conversation. [C] The females lie their eggs in slits that they cut into thin branches. [D] When the eggs hatch, the tiny nymphs drop to the ground and burrow down to begin the long wait again.

[4] Emerging all at once is itself an ambush. Birds, squirrels, and even dogs feast on the cicadas, but there are far more insects than predators can eat, so most survive long enough to breed. By contrast, an insect that emerged a week early or a year late would face the predators alone.

[5] The length of the cycle may matter too. Thirteen and seventeen are prime numbers, divisible only by themselves and one. Some biologists argue that a prime-numbered cycle makes it unlikely that cicadas will emerge in the same year as a predator whose numbers rise and fall on a shorter cycle. A brood on a twelve-year cycle would meet predators on two-, three-, four-, or six-year cycles every time it emerged, a thirteen-year brood would rarely meet any of them.

PASSAGE P8: Face in the Water
=====================================================

[1] I was thirty-four the first time I put my face in the water on purpose. Growing up in a desert town, I had never learned to swim, and as an adult I had become expert at avoiding the subject. I stayed on the beach towel, offered to watch everyones' bags, and laughed when friends asked whether I was coming in.

[2] The adult class at the community pool met on Tuesday nights. There were six of us, a retired bus driver, two college students, a nurse, a man who had recently moved from Nepal, and me. The nurse had nearly drowned as a child; the bus driver simply wanted to keep up with his grandchildren. Our instructor, Paloma, was perhaps twenty years old and spoke to us with the urgency of someone talking a cat out of a tree.

[3] The first lesson had nothing to do with swimming. Paloma asked us to stand in the shallow end, take a breath, and lower our faces into the water while blowing bubbles through our noses. It sounds simple. For me it was terrifying, my body was convinced that the water would rush in and never leave.

[4] By the fourth week, I could float on my back if Paloma kept a hand under my shoulders. By the sixth, she took the hand away without telling me, and I floated on my own for nearly a minute before I noticed. When I realized what had happened, I panicked, swallowed half the pool, and came up coughing and laughing.

[5] I am still not a mighty swimmer. I swim slow, my kick is uneven, and I need to rest at the end of every length. But last summer, for the first time, I left the towel on the sand and walked into the lake with everyone else. Paloma would probably call that a passing grade.
