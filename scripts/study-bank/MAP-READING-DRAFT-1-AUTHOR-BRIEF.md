# MAP Growth Reading, draft batch 1: author brief

You are drafting **four complete reading passage sets** for a MAP Growth Reading practice bank,
grades 5-8. Each set is ONE short passage and its FOUR four-choice questions. An experienced tutor
will hand-edit every set afterwards (about ten minutes each), mostly fixing answer choices that give
the answer away. Write each set as well as you can, as a single passage, and do not over-engineer.
Your job is a strong first draft that reads like a real published grade-level reading test.

**Work only from this brief, your own writing, and the banned-topics list below. Do not open any other
file in the repository, do not use the web, and never open, search for or imitate any NWEA/MAP item
or any PDF.**

## Your four passages

Your assignment (passage ids, genre, grade, target RIT band, word range) is given in the task message.
Grade sizing:

| grade | words (hard limits) | target RIT band(s) | reading level |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 170-260 | 190-199 or 200-209 | grade-5 vocabulary, mostly simple and compound sentences |
| 6 | 200-300 | 200-209 | grade-6 |
| 7 | 240-340 | 200-209 or 210-219 | grade-7 |
| 8 | 280-380 | 210-219 | grade-8 |

Poems may run 120-260 words, set in lines (lines separated by "\n", stanzas by a blank line).
Paragraphs are separated by "\n\n". Each passage has a short **title** (for the editor only).

- **Original prose (or verse) only.** Invented people and places are fine and usually better. Keep
  facts a reader could know from the outside world out of the answers: no question may be answerable
  from general knowledge (earlier drafts leaked through things like US time zones and Dutch flood
  control).
- **Naturalness matters most.** Earlier agent passages were rated "assembled rather than written".
  The known tells, all banned:
  - **filler negations**: sentences that exist to say what did NOT happen or is NOT there. At most one
    negation word (not, no, never, nothing, nor, none, neither, n't, nobody, nowhere) per paragraph, and
    never a run or list of rejected possibilities;
  - **endings that list feelings** the character does or does not have, or that announce the moral
    ("I was not sad, not angry, only..."; "And that is when I learned what friendship means");
  - template rhythm and repeated anchor words (the same key noun hammered in every paragraph so a
    question can find it);
  - a paragraph or sentence that exists only so a question can be asked about it. Every sentence
    belongs to the story or the explanation.
- Write for the grade: concrete, specific, with a real voice, the way a good children's magazine or
  grade-level anthology piece reads. Not a textbook summary.

## The four questions (exactly four per passage, in an order that follows the passage)

Every passage gets:

1. **central idea / theme** (`central-idea` for informational, `theme` for literary): "Which sentence
   best states the central idea of the passage?", "Which statement best expresses a theme of the
   story?", "Which is the best summary of the passage?" (vary the stem).
2. **one text-evidence question**, either `detail` ("According to the passage, why did ...") or
   `inference` ("What can the reader infer about ...", "Which conclusion is best supported by ...").
   For literary passages prefer a character inference (motive, change, relationship).
3. **`vocabulary-in-context`**: "In paragraph N, what does the word "X" mean?" or "Which word or
   phrase means the same as "X" as it is used in paragraph N?" X must be a word a grade-level student
   might genuinely not know or might misread (a multiple-meaning word, or a word whose meaning the
   sentence supports). Never a transparent word (earlier drafts used "because" for grade 5 and
   "creaked" for grade 7, and both were rated far too easy). The four choices are meanings a reader
   could plausibly read INTO THIS SENTENCE, same part of speech, each able to replace X grammatically;
   exactly one is supported by the context. Do not use unrelated dictionary senses that nobody would
   pick in this sentence (a musical scale in a map sentence was rated a dead option), and do not make
   the key the only option that reads like a dictionary gloss.
4. **one craft question**, chosen so your four sets do not all use the same one: `purpose` (why the
   author includes a paragraph, detail or quotation), `structure` (how the passage or a part is
   organised, e.g. problem-solution, cause-effect, comparison, flashback), `point-of-view` (narrator's
   point of view, or the author's view of a subject), or `figurative` (what a simile/metaphor/image
   shows). Use at least three different craft kinds across your four sets.

Four choices each, **exactly one correct**. Difficulty labels relative to the passage's grade:
`easy`, `medium`, `hard`. Aim per passage for one easy, two medium, one hard. Earlier drafts were rated
"easier than target" far more often than "harder": a key that repeats the passage's wording nearly
word for word is too easy. Paraphrase the key; make the reader connect two places in the passage.

### What a good wrong answer looks like

Wrong answers students choose are built from **things the passage really says, placed wrongly**.
Wrong answers nobody chooses are "plausible-sounding but never mentioned" or plainly irrelevant at the
grade level. Use these lure kinds, at least two different kinds per question:

| kind | what it is |
|---|---|
| stops short | the literal surface event, stopping before the inference asked for |
| reversed | cause and effect, or the order of two real events, swapped |
| half right | one element right, one wrong (right person, wrong reason) |
| detail as whole | a real, vivid detail or one paragraph's topic offered as the central idea or purpose |
| misplaced detail | a real detail attached to the wrong person, thing, moment or relation |
| character, not author | what a character believes, where the question asks what the author shows |

Every wrong answer is wrong **by one checkable fact** a careful reader can point to, and is never
"arguably also right". A wrong answer must not be killed by one sentence that flatly denies it word for
word (graders called that "direct negation of one sentence" and rated the item too easy).

### Choices: avoid the giveaways solvers use without the passage

Readers who never see the passage, or even the question, often pick the key from the four choices
alone. The patterns that did it before:
- the keys across the four questions tell one consistent story, and each distractor tells a different
  one. Let distractors share the passage's real people, places and events, so they fit the same world
  as the key, and let at least one distractor per question agree with the other questions' content;
- the key is the only option that covers everything (central idea), or uses a stock purpose phrase
  ("to show how...", "to explain why...") while the others describe content literally;
- the key is the only warm, balanced, moderate or "lesson-like" option, or reads as the
  literary-analysis answer, while the others are absolutes, narrow details, or summaries that add
  invented events;
- the key is the longest or most qualified option;
- a distractor contradicts itself, or is silly at the grade level;
- the same distractor family appears in two questions of one set.

So: all four choices are the **same kind of thing in one grammatical frame**, of similar length
(longest at most 1.5x the shortest, in characters), each one plain claim, none hedged or compound.
One clean pattern from the pilots: put all four options in one frame ("The story shows that X matters
more than Y") and vary only the content the question is about.

## Explanations

- `explanation`: one or two sentences that quote the key's text in double quotes and the passage words
  that make it right. **Never refer to answer letters** (choices are reordered later).
- `distractor_rationales`: for EACH of the three wrong choices, `{ "choice": "<exact text>", "lure":
  "<lure kind>", "reason": "<quotes the choice and the passage words that make it wrong>" }`.
- `why`: a span copied verbatim from the passage that supports the key.

## File format

Write ONE JSON file (the path you are given) holding an array of your four sets:

```json
[
  {
    "passage_id": "M1-P01",
    "title": "...",
    "genre": "fiction | poetry | folktale | memoir | science | history | biography | how-it-works | opinion",
    "text_type": "literary | informational",
    "grade": 5,
    "target_band": "RIT 190-199",
    "passage": "full text with \n\n between paragraphs",
    "items": [
      {
        "qid": "M1-P01-1",
        "kind": "theme",
        "map_area": "Literary Text | Informational Text | Vocabulary",
        "difficulty": "medium",
        "prompt": "Which statement best expresses a theme of the story?",
        "choices": ["...", "...", "...", "..."],
        "correct_answer": "<exact text of one choice>",
        "why": "verbatim span",
        "explanation": "...",
        "distractor_rationales": [ {"choice": "...", "lure": "...", "reason": "..."} ]
      }
    ]
  }
]
```

`map_area`: vocabulary items are `Vocabulary`; the others take the passage's area (`Literary Text` or
`Informational Text`). qids are `<passage_id>-1` to `-4` in the order students see them. Put the key
anywhere in the list; the orchestrator reorders every set's choices afterwards. Paragraph numbers in
stems count "\n\n"-separated paragraphs (for a poem, say "line N" or "stanza N").
