PASSAGE P1: Sixpence a Book
=====================================================

[1] In 1934 the publisher Allen Lane spent a weekend in Devon with the novelist Agatha Christie. Waiting at Exeter station for the train back to London, he searched the bookstall for something to read and found only glossy magazines and reprints of forgettable novels. Good books existed, of course, but in Britain a new hardback cost about seven shillings. That was well beyond what most travelers would invest on a few hours of reading.

[2] Lane's answer was simple. He would sell good books, novels and biographies that had already succeeded in hardback, for sixpence each, roughly the price of a pack of cigarettes. Other publishers thought the plan was generous. If serious books cost so little, they argued, readers would stop buying the expensive editions, and authors would earn almost nothing.

[3] Lane went ahead anyway. The first ten Penguins appeared in the summer of 1935 their covers divided into three horizontal bands: orange for fiction, green for crime, and blue for biography. The design was plain, it was also easy to recognize from across a shop. Lane did not wait for bookshops to come around. Instead, he persuaded the chain store Woolworths to place a large order, and the books were soon stacked beside the kitchenware and the sweets.

[4] The gamble paid off. Sales of the first ten titles was strong enough that Penguin sold three million books within a year, and rival publishers who had predicted ruin began launching cheap series of their own. During the Second World War, special editions went to the armed forces. Printed in special editions small enough for a uniform pocket, soldiers carried paperbacks from camp to camp and passed them along until the pages came loose.

[5] Penguin's success did more than make a fortune for one publisher. It changed who could own a library. Clerks and factory workers could now buy a novel every week out of their spare change and keep it on a shelf at home. Ninety years later, the bookstall that disappointed Lane is long gone, but the idea he had there is on sale in stations around the world.

PASSAGE P2: Now You See It
=====================================================

[1] A common octopus resting on a reef can vanish in less than a second. Its skin shifts from rusty red to the mottled gray-green of the rock beneath it, and small bumps rise on its surface to mock the texture of algae. Few animals can change their appearance so completely or so quickly.

[2] The trick begins with cells called chromatophores. Each one is a tiny elastic sac of pigment—yellow, red, brown, or black, ringed by muscles. When the muscles contract, they stretch the sac wide, and the pigment spreads across a larger patch of skin. When the muscles relax, the sac shrinks back to a dot that is almost invisible. Because each of these muscles are wired to nerves from the brain, an octopus can open thousands of sacs at once.

[3] Beneath the chromatophores lies two other layers of cells. Iridophores reflect light to produce shimmering blues and greens, and leucophores scatter all colors equally, giving the skin a bright white background. Working together, the three layers allow an octopus to produce an enormous range of patterns, from stripes and spots to flickering bands of color.

[4] What puzzles biologists is that the octopus appears to be colorblind. Its eyes contain only one type of light-sensitive pigment, which should let it see brightness but not hue. Similarly, it matches the color of its surroundings with remarkable accuracy.

[5] Researchers have proposed two explanations, and they are not mutually exclusive. One is that the skin itself can sense light: proteins similar to those in the eye have been found in octopus skin. The other concerns the animal's oddly shaped pupil, which may bring different colors into focus at slightly different distances and so give the brain clues about hue. Neither idea has yet been proven, the octopus's disappearing act remains, at least in part, a mystery.

PASSAGE P3: Forty Seconds
=====================================================

[1] The summer I turned sixteen, I took a job at Delgado Hardware a narrow shop wedged between a laundromat and a shoe-repair stand. On my first morning, Mr. Delgado handed me a canvas apron and pointed at the key-cutting machine in the back corner. "That," he said, "is the most important machine in the store."

[2] I thought he was joking. The machine looked like a greasy vise bolted to a grinding wheel, and it accomplished only one feat. A customer handed me a key; I clamped it beside a blank, and a metal guide traced the old key's ridges while the wheel cut the same shape into the new one. The whole process took about forty seconds.

[3] For the first week I cut keys badly. Some came out with burrs so sharp they snagged the lining of a pocket, and others turned stiffly in the lock or not at all. Mr. Delgado never scolds me. Therefore, he would take each failed key, run his thumb along its edge, and hand it back without a word, and I would try again.

[4] By August I had cut hundreds of keys, and I had started to notice who needed them. A woman whose husband had just moved out wanted three copies, one for each of her children. A college student bought a key to his first apartment and held it up to the light as if it were a medal. An elderly man asked for a spare because he kept locking himself out, and he laughed about it, but his hands shook as he paid.

[5] On my last day Mr. Delgado asked me whether I finally understood about the machine. I told him I did. A key, I had learned, rarely just a piece of metal. It is usually a sign that something in someone's life is beginning, ending, or getting harder to manage, and for forty seconds at a time I had been part of it.

PASSAGE P4: Paths Nobody Drew
=====================================================

[1] Walk across almost any college campus and you will find them: narrow strips of bare dirt cutting diagonally across the lawns. Planners call these trails "desire lines" or "desire paths." No one designs them. They appear because hundreds of people, each acting alone, decide that the paved walkway takes too long and step onto the grass. The next person sees the flattened strip and follows it.

[2] [A] For decades, the usual response was to fight them. Groundskeepers put up fences, planted hedges, and posted signs asking people to stay on the walkways. [B] As a result, these measures rarely worked for long. [C] A fence would be stepped over and a hedge trampled. [D] Within a season the dirt line would return, often in exactly the same place.

[3] Some planners have taken the identical approach. Rather than deciding in advance where people should walk, they wait to see where people actually do. At a few universities, new lawns have been left unpaved for a year after construction. Once the desire paths have formed, workers pave them the result is a network of walkways that matches how the campus is really used.

[4] The idea has spread beyond campuses. City officials in several countries has studied desire paths in parks and around transit stations, treating each worn trail as a kind of vote for a route that no one planned. A path that cuts behind a bus shelter for instance, may reveal that the official crossing is too far from the stop.

[5] Desire paths are not always a sign of poor design. Sometimes a shortcut damages tree roots or crossing a slope that erodes easily, and a barrier is the right answer. But planners who read them carefully have learned something useful. The people who use a space every day often know it better than the people who drew it.
