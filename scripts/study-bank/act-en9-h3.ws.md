PASSAGE P13: The Way Back Upstream
=====================================================

[1] A young salmon leaves the stream where it hatched when it is only a few inches long. It travels downstream to the sea, where it may spend several years feeding and growing larger in size. Then, when it is ready to spawn, it does something that has puzzled observers for centuries. Swimming back, against the current, to the very stream where it began.

[2] Scientists now suspect that the journey depends on two different senses, one for the open ocean and one for the final stretch. Far from land, a salmon appears to steer by the Earth's magnetic field. The strength and angle of that field shifts gradually from place to place, so every stretch of coastline carries a slightly different magnetic signature. A fish that registers the signature of its home rivers mouth on the way out can, in principle, steer back toward it years later.

[3] Near the coast, a second sense takes over. [A] While it is still young, a salmon memorizes the scent of its home stream, a chemical blend produced by the soil, plants, and rocks of one watershed. [B] Years later, the returning adult tests the water at every fork in the river, following that address upstream one branch at a time. [C] Biologists call this early learning imprinting. [D]

[4] In the 1950s, a team of biologists, working on a creek in the Pacific Northwest, tested the idea by plugging the noses of salmon caught on their way upstream. Released below a fork in the creek, the biologists found that those fish chose the wrong branch far more often than salmon whose noses had been left open.

[5] Reaching the right stream matters. Most Pacific salmon die within days of spawning, so each fish has a single chance to leave offspring. Even so, a stream's own salmon are suited to it: over many generations, each population has adapted to the temperature, current, and seasonal timing of its home water. A fish that ends up elsewhere may arrive in the wrong season or find gravel unsuited to its eggs.

[6] The system is not flawless. A small share of every run strays into neighboring streams, and these wanderers are probably how salmon colonize new rivers, such as those uncovered by retreating glaciers.

PASSAGE P14: A Penny to Anywhere
=====================================================

[1] Before 1840, mailing a letter in Britain was costly and confusing. The charges, which rose with every mile a letter traveled and every extra sheet it held, were usually paid by the person who received the letter rather than by the one who sent it. A letter crossing the length of the country could cost more than a shilling, a large share of many workers' daily pay.

[2] Many families found ways to avoid the charge. Because the recipient paid, a sender could mark the outside of a letter with a prearranged sign, such as a dot in one corner or an underlined name. The person at the other end would glance at the sign, learn the news it carried, and hand the letter back unopened, paying nothing. Others sent letters with travelers, this practice broke the law but was rarely punished.

[3] A former schoolteacher named Rowland Hill had helped run his family's school before he turned to other work. Hill laid out his plan in 1837. He did so in a pamphlet called Post Office Reform. In it, he showed that the real cost of a letter lay in receiving it, sorting it, and to deliver it, not in carrying it. Because distance mattered so little, he proposed a single rate of one penny for any letter up to half an ounce, paid in advance by the sender.

[4] The reform changed three things at once; who paid, how the price was figured, and how often ordinary people could afford to write. The Uniform Penny Post began on January 10, 1840, and the Penny Black, the first adhesive postage stamp, went on sale that May. Within a year, the number of letters passing through the mail had roughly doubled.

[5] Letter writing itself began to change. Under the old rates, thrifty writers had often filled a page, turned it sideways, and written a second layer of lines across the first rather than pay for another sheet. However, a letter was now charged by weight, so a writer could usually add a second page at no extra cost. Envelopes, which had once counted as an extra sheet, begin to appear in shops everywhere. Valentines now cost a penny to send, and stationers soon printed it by the thousands.

PASSAGE P15: Ready About
=====================================================

[1] You arrive at the dock earlier than you need to, wearing a borrowed life jacket and repeating the words you studied the night before: tiller, boom, mainsheet, jib. The boat your instructor points to is about the length of a car, and it rocks when you step near it. The harbor's sailing club, which owns the boat, was founded in the 1920s by a group of local fishermen. Odile, who has taught sailing on this harbor for thirty years, settles into the front of the boat and hands you the tiller.

[2] Her first lesson is that the boat does the opposite of what your hand suggests. Push the tiller to the left, and the bow swings to the right. You know this rule; you read it twice last night. Your arm does not know it, and for ten minutes you zigzag across the harbor like a shopping cart with a bad wheel.

[3] Then a gust arrives. The wind presses on the sail from the side. Similarly, the boat leans until the water is a few inches from the rail. You grip the edge of the seat and laugh at how quickly the shore slides past. Odile only nods, and the look that passes between you and her says that every new sailor grabs that same seat.

[4] Next comes tacking: turning the boat's nose through the wind so the sail fills on the other side. When Odile calls the warning, you push the tiller toward the sail the boat swings its nose across the wind. The most dangerous part of the turn—the second when the sail goes slack, arrives without much warning. The heavy, aluminum boom, swings low across the cockpit, and you duck under it a half second before it would have found your head.

[5] By the sixth tack, the boat feels real steady under you, and Odile has stopped calling out corrections. She tells you that the best sailors she knows capsized most often in their first summer, and that next week you will tip the boat over on purpose. An hour later you sail the boat back to the dock by yourself, on your own, while she sits in the bow and finishes her coffee.
