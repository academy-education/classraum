PASSAGE P1: The Sign Shop
=====================================================

[1] The summer I turned fifteen, my parents sent me to stay with my uncle Teodor, who ran a sign shop out of a garage in Marlow Falls. I had pictured computers and vinyl cutters. What I found instead was a long wooden bench a radio tuned to baseball, and rows of brushes standing in coffee cans like bouquets.

[2] Teodor painted every letter by hand, and at first I could not see why anyone paid him to. His customers could have bought printed banners for half the price: the bakery on Main Street, a family of beekeepers, a dentist who wanted his name in gold leaf. On my first morning I noticed that the jars of paint on the shelf above his bench was labeled in his own careful capitals, as if even his supplies deserved good lettering.

[3] He started me on the alphabet, I painted capital A's for three days. My first A's leaned like tired fence posts. Teodor would choose the least terrible in each row and circle it with a grease pencil. For two days I could not tell his circled letters from the rest. In other words, I began to see what he was choosing: the letters whose two strokes met cleanly at the peak and stood evenly on the line. A letter, he told me, stands the way a building does: if their base is crooked, nothing above it will look straight.

[4] By August, Teodor trusted me to repaint the bakery's front window, where the old gold letters had faded in the sun until they were barely visible and could hardly be seen. I traced their faint outlines onto paper before scraping the glass clean. Then I worked so slowly that the baker came out twice to ask whether I was still alive.

[5] By the end of the summer, I had lettered three customers' signs on my own: a truck door for the beekeepers, a price board for the hardware store, and a brass plaque for the dentist. Not one of them is perfect. Now, whenever I pass a hand-painted sign, I look first at the bottom of the letters. Checking, the way Teodor taught me, whether they stand evenly on the line. Usually I can tell which painters had someone circling the good ones.

PASSAGE P2: Skin That Changes
=====================================================

[1] An octopus resting on a reef can disappear in under a second. One moment it is a pale, smooth lump on the sand; the next it has taken on the mottled brown of the rock beside it, along with the rock's bumps and ridges. Few animals can alter both their color and their texture, and none can match the octopus for speed.

[2] The color comes from thousands of tiny organs called chromatophores. Each one a stretchy sac of red, yellow, brown, or black pigment ringed by muscle. When the muscles contract they pull the sac open, spreading the pigment across a wider patch of skin, and the sac shrinks back to a speck as soon as they relax. Because these muscles are controlled directly by nerves, an octopus can send a new pattern rippling across it's whole body in less time than a blink takes.

[3] Octopuses are also among the most intelligent of all invertebrates. Iridophores reflect light in shimmering blues and greens, the way a soap bubble does. Leucophores, scatter every color equally, so they look white under white light and take on the tint of whatever light falls on them. With these layers working together, an octopus can match not only the shade of its surroundings but also their brightness.

[4] Color alone would not hide an octopus against a crusted rock. [A] These are small bands of muscle in the skin that, when they tighten, push the surface up into bumps, ridges, or branching spikes. [B] Within seconds, a smooth animal can grow a coat of points that resembles the coral around it. [C] Covered in such spikes, a diver can swim within arm's reach of an octopus and never see it. [D] When the threat passed, the muscles relax and the skin lies flat again.

[5] What puzzles biologists is that octopuses appear to be colorblind. Their eyes contain only one kind of light receptor, yet they match colors they should not be able to tell apart. PASSAGE P3: Reading in the Dark
=====================================================

[1] In the early 1800s, a French artillery captain wanted a way for soldiers to pass messages at night without striking a light that would reveal their position. His answer was "night writing," a code of raised dots punched into heavy paper and read with the fingertips. Each symbol stood for a sound rather than a letter, and a single symbol could use as many as twelve dots.

[2] The army never adopted the code. Soldiers found it slow to learn, and a message of a few words could take minutes to read. The captain then offered his invention to the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris. One of the first students to try it, in 1821, was a twelve-year-old named Louis Braille, who had lost his sight in an accident in his father's workshop.

[3] Braille saw at once that the code was clever but clumsy. A fingertip could not take in a twelve-dot symbol at one touch, so a reader had to slide back and forth over each one. Over the next three years, often working late at night, he cut the cell down to six dots; two columns of three. His father, a harness maker, had hoped the boy would one day take over the family workshop. The number of distinct patterns a six-dot cell can form are sixty-three, enough for the whole alphabet and common punctuation, and a fingertip can feel all six dots at once.

[4] Braille published his system in 1829, when he was twenty, and figured the school would totally love it. The school's staff, however, resisted it. Many sighted teachers preferred raised versions of printed letters, since reading them by eye, correcting them without special training, and to teach them required no new code. For a time the school even banned braille from its classrooms, but students went on passing it to one another in the dormitories.

[5] Braille died of tuberculosis in 1852 he was only forty-three. Two years later, France adopts his system as the official method for teaching blind students. Today a reader, who runs a finger along a line of braille, is still touching a soldier's idea, cut down to the size of a fingertip.

PASSAGE P5: Building the Grid
=====================================================

[1] Before a crossword reaches a newspaper, it begins as an empty square, drawn on graph paper or, more often now, on a computer screen. The person who fills it, is called a constructor, and the work is closer to engineering than most solvers suspect.

[2] A constructor starts with the theme entries, the long answers that stretch across the middle of the grid. Those entries are placed first. Nevertheless, every other square must eventually be built around them, both across and down. In a puzzle about weather, the theme entries might be phrases that each hide a kind of storm, and that hidden thread is what gives a puzzle their personality.

[3] The grid itself follows strict rules, every white square must belong to both an across answer and a down answer, and no answer may be shorter than three letters. The pattern of black squares must also look the same when the grid is turned upside down. Placing the black squares by hand, the symmetry is the first thing a constructor checks.

[4] Then comes the fill; the hundreds of shorter words that lock the theme entries together. Here software has changed the craft more than any other tool. A program can suggest thousands of words that fit a given pattern of letters, and it ranks them in order by how familiar and well-known they are likely to be. Yet the constructor still decides. A program will happily offer the name of an obscure Siberian river; a good constructor would rather rebuild an entire corner than make solvers guess at it.

[5] Once every square has been filled, the constructor turns to the clues, a separate art with its own rules. Some constructors fill their grids by hand, and others lean on software. Of the two methods, filling by hand is the slower, but its devotees say it produces grids with more character. Either way, the finished puzzle will be solved in perhaps twenty minutes by people who never think about the black squares at all.

=====================================================

[1] For most of my life I have told people that I could not sing. I said it the way other people mention an allergy, as a plain fact about my body that no one could argue with. In second grade a music teacher asks me to "just mouth the words" during a holiday concert, and I took her advice for the next thirty years.

[2] Then last fall my neighbor Dolores, a longtime patron of the Eastfield Community Chorus, invited me to a rehearsal. I told her my usual line. She told me that the chorus had exactly one rule for joining, and it was showing up.

[3] The chorus, I soon learned, was not a gathering of polished voices. Our members came from nearly every neighborhood in Eastfield. The director a retired band teacher named Mr. Achterberg, never asked anyone to audition; instead, he listened to each newcomer speak for a moment and then pointed toward a section. He put me with the basses, in the back row, two rows behind Dolores and the other altos.

[4] During my first rehearsal I stood perfectly still and moved my lips, exactly as I had in second grade. The hardest part of those first weeks was not the notes but the silence between them, when I was sure everyone could hear that I was not singing. What changed it was the other basses' patience. They never once corrected me; they simply sang a little louder whenever I came in late, so that I could follow them.

[5] By December I was singing out loud, not well, but out loud, Mr. Achterberg noticed it before I did. He told Dolores and I that the basses finally sounded like a section. I still would not call myself a singer. But on concert night, standing in the back row, I sang the words—all of them, even the ones I was unsure of, instead of mouthing them.
