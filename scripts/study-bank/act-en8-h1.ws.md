=====================================================

[1] A tardigrade is easy to overlook. PASSAGE P3: The Day the Railroads Set the Clocks
=====================================================

[1] Until the 1880s, most American towns kept their own time. Noon was the moment the sun stood highest over the town, so noon in one city arrived a few minutes earlier or later than noon in the next. For people who rarely traveled far from home, the differences hardly mattered.

[2] The railroads changed that. A passenger heading west from Boston passed through dozens of local times, and each railroad company kept its own time as well, usually that of the city where it had its headquarters. A large station might display several clocks, each showing a different hour for a different line. Boston's clocks, set each day by a signal from a nearby observatory, were among the most accurate in the country. Timetables became puzzles that required arithmetic, worse, schedules that disagreed by even a few minutes made collisions on single-track lines more likely.

[3] Railroad managers, who had argued about reform for years, finally acted in 1883. The plan they adopted divided the country into four time zones. Each zone was roughly fifteen degrees of longitude wide. Within a zone, every railroad clock would show the same time. On November 18, 1883 railroad clocks across the country were reset at noon. Operators sent out telegraph signals simultaneously at the very same instant, and station clocks were adjusted to match. In cities east of a zone's center, clocks were turned back several minutes, so residents heard the noon bells ring twice.

[4] Not everyone welcomed the change. Some newspapers complained that the railroads had done something no government had ever dared, they had rearranged the sun. A few cities kept their local time for years. Similarly, the convenience of a single shared hour won out, and holdout towns set their clocks by the railroad. In 1918, Congress made the zones law.

[5] Today the arrangement seems as natural as the weather. Few people who check the time on a phone know that the lines dividing one hour from the next were drawn first on railroad maps.

PASSAGE P6: Wire by Wire
=====================================================

[1] A suspension bridge hangs its deck from two main cables, and those cables carry the deck's weight over the towers to anchorages set in rock or concrete on each shore. On a large bridge, each cable may be more than half a meter thick. A cable cannot be made in a factory and lifted into place, because at its full, complete size it would be far too heavy and weighty to hoist.
