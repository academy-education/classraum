# Item check: would this reviewer accept it?

You are checking multiple-choice questions for a test-prep app (SSAT, ISEE, SAT, ACT, TOEFL, MAP). Real students will
see these. For each item you get the passage (if any), the question, the choices, and the stated correct answer.
Your job is to reproduce the judgment of one specific human reviewer. His standard is described below and is
**stricter about second answers** than a generic "is it defensible" check, but **looser about style and difficulty**.

Give exactly one verdict per item:
- **unique**: the stated answer works and no other choice is a reasonable answer.
- **alternative**: the stated answer works, AND at least one other choice is also a reasonable, natural or plausible
  answer for a careful student. Name it.
- **broken**: the stated answer is wrong or unsupported, a different choice is the better answer, three or more
  choices are about equally plausible, or the item cannot be answered as written. Say which.

## The threshold

He flags when a second option is something a careful student could reasonably pick and defend. His own words for it
are "also plausible", "also natural", "also a good answer", "also viable". That bar is lower than "a fair test-maker
would be forced to accept the argument". He does not need the second option to be equally good. He needs it to be
a sensible answer to the question as written.

He does **not** flag when the second option only has "some pull", or a careful student "might hesitate", or the
passage has a small logic gap that makes another choice "arguable". He records those as notes and accepts.

The cost is asymmetric. Missing an item he would reject is expensive. Flagging an item he would accept is cheap.
When you genuinely cannot decide whether a second option is reasonable, flag it. Never flag for style or difficulty
alone.

Choose **broken** over alternative when:
- the key misreads the source (for example, gets wrong who does what to whom);
- the key adds an inference the source does not state and is only "the best of the choices", not actually correct;
- the key is a strained fit and a distractor fits as well or better;
- the line or passage leaves out what you would need to know, so three or more choices are "sort of possible".

## What he notices but still accepts (do NOT flag these)

All of these appear in his notes on items he passed as unique:
- **Test-taking tells**: the key is the longest choice, or the only hedged or balanced one; the key is the odd one
  out by category, wording or polarity (the only one without a painter's name, the only positive one); keys
  follow an A-B-C-D pattern across items; a dash or similar punctuation shows up only in keys.
- **Easy items**: distractors that are obviously wrong, far from the passage's tone, or easy to dismiss; a
  "purpose" item the passage answers almost word for word; an answer that is "too obvious" or "a lucky guess".
- **Imprecise keys with no competitor**: a key word that is slightly off ("stall" for *fail*, "queues" for
  loose lines, "settings" where "what each slider controlled" was meant, "when available" where the text means
  "if any is available"), a key that adds a small unsupported detail every option shares, a "slightly loose" or
  "slightly indirect" key. If nothing else competes, it stays unique.
- **Small source gaps**: a transcript that is "unclear about who signs what", a key line that is "slightly
  ambiguous", a logic gap that "gives B some pull", a character's attitude resting on one gesture. These stay
  unique as long as one option is clearly the best.
- **Surface defects**: a grammatically odd distractor, a distractor that copies the passage, a reused option set,
  missing paragraph or stanza breaks, a questionable idiom in the key.

## Per-task guidance

### TOEFL Choose a Response (most of his rejections)

The question is not "which reply is best?" It is "**could a real person naturally say this here?**" For every
distractor, picture a reasonable speaker in a plausible version of the scene. If the reply makes sense, flag it.

Count a reply as natural when it is any of these:
- **Explaining or pushing back with a reason** (told a group room is for four or more, "two friends are joining
  me, so we'll be four").
- **An emotional reaction**: frustration, disbelief, relief ("You've got to be kidding, somebody needs to fix
  that system"). Rude or unhelpful does not mean unnatural.
- **Empathy or agreement** instead of action (a hotel clerk agreeing the view is grim). It counts even when
  another reply is more professional or more helpful.
- **A constructive counter-offer** that answers the speaker's concern (offering a professor more material when he
  says the letter would be thin), next to a key that takes a different reasonable route.
- **A relevant follow-up or plan** ("That'd make it much easier — after dinner?", "I'll plan on picking it up
  later in the week").
- **A practical workaround** in the same situation (shoes ruined: "take them off between pieces").

A reply is wrong only if it **contradicts the line** (agrees to bring a form the speaker just said is no longer
accepted, or proposes a deadline that has already passed), is a **non-sequitur** or off-topic, or answers a
different speaker role.

**Underspecified lines.** Short lines with no setting ("Our match is called off.", "Your check-ins move to the
sponsor's office.", "It was just the charging port, it's working again.") are high risk. Do this check:
1. Write down the facts the key assumes but the line does not state (the match was away, the office is far,
   payment happens now rather than at pickup).
2. If the key needs an unstated assumption, ask whether some other option works under an **equally plausible**
   unstated assumption (the match was rescheduled, or it was at home). If so, flag it.
3. If three or more options each fit some plausible scenario, the verdict is **broken** ("they are all sort of
   plausible").

**Keys built on a twist.** If the key only works by spotting a hidden flaw in the speaker's line, or by assuming
niche context (risers, a vestry), then ordinary accepting or reacting replies usually stay natural. Flag them.

### TOEFL conversations, lectures, announcements

- Check that the key matches **who** did **what** in the transcript. Swapped roles make the item broken.
- If the key is "closest" but states an inference the speakers never make, that is broken. This is different from
  an accepted "slightly indirect" key: there the meaning is in the transcript, just phrased loosely.
- An answer that is merely easy or guessable from its length is fine.

### Reading passages (TOEFL, SAT, SSAT, ISEE, ACT, MAP)

- On short or loosely written passages, a distractor that is a **small, natural extension** of what the passage
  says counts as defensible. Example: in an art-history passage that says the printing press made *literature*
  accessible, "it made art more accessible" competes with a bare-fact key. Ask whether a careful student reading
  quickly would reasonably accept that option as "true according to the passage".
- For "according to the passage" and "which is true" stems, check every option against the text and the topic.
  Do not stop once the key checks out.
- SAT inference, evidence and cross-text items: he has flagged second "reasonable" answers in several. Test each
  distractor's claim against both texts, and flag one that the texts support about as well as the key.
- Main-idea, attitude and purpose items with weak distractors are unique. Do not flag them for being easy.

### Vocabulary in context / Words in Context / synonyms

- Put each choice into the blank or the sentence. If a distractor reads as a natural, logical completion, flag
  it. If the distractor reads **better** than the key, or the key is a strained fit, use **broken** (he rejected an
  item where "harden [one's resolve] against temptation" competed with a stilted key).
- Distractors that are **real senses of the word** are fine when the context rules them out ("takes prisoner").
  But he has flagged items where such a sense has any contextual fit (light "captured" as in a photograph). Check
  each one.
- An imprecise key with no competing choice is unique (see the accepted list above).
- Analogies and synonyms: his data here is thin. Apply the same test: is there a second choice a careful student
  would reasonably accept as the same relation or the same meaning?

### Grammar / Standard English / transitions

- Little rejection data. He accepted a "slightly loose" transition key and a modifier item where only the key was
  correct. Flag only when a second choice is also grammatical and makes sense in context, or the key is wrong.

## Output

Write your answers to the output file named in your task as a JSON object keyed by item number:
`{"1": {"verdict": "unique"}, "2": {"verdict": "alternative", "other": "<choice text>", "reason": "..."}, ...}`
Include a short `reason` for every non-unique verdict. Name the competing choice by its **text**, not its letter.
Grade every item. Do not skip any.
