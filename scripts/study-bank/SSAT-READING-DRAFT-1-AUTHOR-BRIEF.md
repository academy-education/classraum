# SSAT Upper Reading, draft batch 1: author brief

You are drafting **four complete SSAT Upper Level reading passage sets**: each is ONE passage and its
SIX five-choice questions. A person (an experienced SSAT tutor) will hand-edit every set afterwards,
about ten minutes each, mostly fixing answer choices that give the answer away. So: write each set as
well as you can, as a single passage (there are no alternative versions), and do not over-engineer.
Your job is a strong first draft that reads like a real published test.

## The passages

- **Length:** 330-520 words each (hard limits 300-600; poems may run 220-400 words, set in lines,
  stanzas separated by a blank line). Paragraphs separated by "\n\n"; lines of a poem separated by "\n".
- **Original prose (or verse)**, at the level of a published SSAT Upper Level passage (grades 8-11):
  specific, concrete, with a real voice. Invented people and places are fine and usually better; keep
  facts that a reader could know from the outside world out of the answers.
- **Naturalness matters most.** Earlier agent passages were rated a median 2 out of 5 for naturalness
  against 5 for real published prose. The known tells, all banned:
  - **filler negations**: sentences that exist to say what did NOT happen or is NOT there ("Nothing
    climbs in that season", "No usher counts the seats", "It was not the flood"). At most one negation
    word (not, no, never, nothing, nor, none, neither, n't, nobody, nowhere) per paragraph, and never a
    run or list of rejected possibilities;
  - **endings that list feelings the narrator does not have**, or announce the moral ("I was not angry,
    not sad, only..."; "And that, I learned, is what home means");
  - template rhythm: "Five explanations were offered: ... One of them does the work.";
  - a paragraph that exists only so a question can be asked about it. Every sentence belongs to the
    story or argument.
- Each passage has a short **title** (it is not shown to students, it is for the editor).

## The six questions (exactly one of each per passage, in an order that follows the passage)

`main-idea`, `detail`, `inference`, `vocabulary-in-context`, `attitude`, `purpose`.

Five choices each, **one correct**. Difficulty labels follow the live bank: main-idea `easy`; detail
and vocabulary `medium`; inference, purpose and attitude `medium` or `hard` (aim for about one easy,
three medium and two hard per passage). Hardness should come from one strong wrong answer built from
real passage content, not from a long chain of reasoning.

### What a good wrong answer looks like

Wrong answers that real students choose are built from **things the passage really says, placed
wrongly**. Wrong answers nobody chooses are "plausible-sounding, but never mentioned". Use these lure
kinds, at least two different kinds per question:

| kind | what it is |
|---|---|
| stops short | the literal surface event, stopping before the inference asked for |
| reversed | cause and effect, or the order of two real events, swapped |
| half right | one element right, one wrong (right person, wrong reason) |
| detail as whole | a real, vivid detail or one paragraph's topic offered as the main idea or purpose |
| misplaced detail | a real detail attached to the wrong person, thing, moment or relation |
| character, not author | what a character believes, where the question asks what the author concludes |

Every wrong answer is wrong **by one checkable fact** a careful reader can point to, and is never
"arguably also right". If a rival explanation is in the passage, make it checkably wrong (it belongs to
a different person, year or decision), not merely less likely.

### Choices: avoid the giveaways solvers use without the passage

Readers who never see the passage, or even the question, can often pick the key from the five choices
alone. The patterns that did it before:
- the key is the only choice that agrees with the OTHER questions' keys (all keys tell one consistent
  story; the distractors each tell a different one). Let distractors share the passage's real people,
  places and events, so they fit the same world as the key;
- the key is the longest, most qualified, most moderate or most "sophisticated" choice; the
  distractors are absolutes or extremes;
- the key is the only choice whose wording varies along the axis the question asks about (e.g. four
  choices name an action and the key names a motive);
- the key is the only warm or balanced attitude among four negative ones.

So: all five choices are the **same kind of thing in one grammatical frame**, of similar length
(longest at most 1.5x the shortest, in characters), each one plain claim, none hedged or compound.

### Per kind

- **Main idea** ("The passage is primarily concerned with", "Which best states the main idea"): at
  least one wrong choice is as broad as the key, so "pick the broadest" never decides it.
- **Detail** ("According to the passage, ..."): lures take the words right next to the answer, or a
  real fact from elsewhere attached to the wrong thing.
- **Inference** ("It can be inferred that", "The passage suggests"): lures are stops-short,
  one-step-too-far, and character-not-author.
- **Purpose** (of a paragraph, a sentence or a detail): all five purposes should be things that part
  COULD be doing; lures are detail-as-whole and reversed function.
- **Vocabulary-in-context** ('As it is used in line/paragraph N, the word "X" most nearly means'):
  - **X comes from YOUR headword list** (given at the end). Use it in a form that reads naturally.
  - The five choices are five **clearly separate senses** of X (separate dictionary entries, not shades
    of one), short glosses, same part of speech, each able to replace X grammatically. No two glosses
    may both fit any ordinary sentence. Before keeping a gloss, try to write one sentence where X could
    carry it AND another of your glosses; if you can, replace one.
  - Exactly one sense fits X's sentence in the passage, and the sentence's own object or setting rules
    out the other four. No rare dictionary senses, no transparent words.
- **Attitude** (author / narrator / speaker form): the stem asks how the **writer** (the essayist, the
  memoir's narrator, the first-person narrator of a story, the poem's speaker) feels about the
  **subject** the stem names, e.g. "The narrator's attitude toward the old ferry is best described as".
  Never a character's reaction.
  - The five choices come from **five different directions**, one word each:

    | direction | allowed words |
    |---|---|
    | warm | admiring, approving, appreciative, proud, respectful, grateful, sympathetic, fond, affectionate, enthusiastic, reverent, tender |
    | critical | critical, disapproving, scornful, contemptuous, indignant, irritated, resentful, exasperated, disdainful, annoyed, angry |
    | anxious | uneasy, worried, apprehensive, anxious, wary, fearful, alarmed, troubled |
    | sorrowful | regretful, sad, wistful, nostalgic, mournful, melancholy, rueful, sorrowful |
    | amused | amused, playful, humorous, whimsical |

    (Noun forms such as "admiration", "regret", "worry" or "amusement" also count, and a short frame
    like "affectionate admiration" is not allowed: one word or one noun per choice.) No irony words
    (ironic, mocking, wry, sarcastic) and no "no feeling" words (detached, neutral, indifferent).
  - The passage licenses **exactly one** direction, through the whole passage, and **two separate full
    sentences** (usually in different paragraphs) each show it on their own. Give both in `why` and
    `why2`, copied exactly. The passage never names the attitude word or any word from the table, and
    no sentence carrying the attitude is ironic, dry or teasing. Do not always key the warm direction:
    across your four sets, key at least two different directions.

## Explanations

- `explanation`: one or two sentences that quote the key's text in double quotes and the passage
  sentence that makes it right, e.g. `"to show that the repair was done in a hurry" is correct: the
  passage says "..."`. **Never refer to answer letters** (choices are reordered later).
- `distractor_rationales`: for EACH of the four wrong choices, `{ "choice": "<exact choice text>",
  "lure": "<lure kind>", "reason": "<quotes the choice text and the passage words that make it wrong>" }`.
- `why`: a span copied verbatim from the passage that makes the key right (attitude: `why` and `why2`,
  two full sentences).

## File format

Write ONE JSON file (the path you are given) holding an array of your four sets:

```json
[
  {
    "passage_id": "D1-P01",
    "title": "...",
    "genre": "fiction | memoir | poetry | history | science | essay",
    "passage": "full text with \n\n between paragraphs",
    "items": [
      {
        "qid": "D1-P01-1",
        "kind": "main-idea",
        "difficulty": "easy",
        "prompt": "The passage is primarily concerned with",
        "choices": ["...", "...", "...", "...", "..."],
        "correct_answer": "<exact text of one choice>",
        "why": "verbatim span",
        "why2": "attitude only: a second verbatim full sentence",
        "explanation": "...",
        "distractor_rationales": [ {"choice": "...", "lure": "...", "reason": "..."} ]
      }
    ]
  }
]
```

qids are `<passage_id>-1` to `-6` in the order students see them. Put the key anywhere in the list;
the orchestrator reorders every set's choices afterwards.

## Self-check before you finish

- `wc -w` each passage (within limits).
- Every `why`, `why2` and quoted passage span is copied exactly; every `correct_answer` and every
  rationale's `choice` matches a choice exactly.
- Choice lengths within 1.5x per question; one attitude word per direction; vocabulary headword from
  your list; no attitude-table word anywhere in the passage.
- Reread each passage start to finish as a stranger: does it read like a published passage? Count
  your negations per paragraph.
- Then cover the passage and the question and look only at each set of five choices: if one choice
  obviously stands out, rewrite the OTHERS so they stand with it.
