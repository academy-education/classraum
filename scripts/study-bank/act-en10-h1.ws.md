PASSAGE P1: Room to Sit
=====================================================

[1] Walk the length of Main Street on a Saturday afternoon and count the places where a person can sit down without buying something. We counted two: the steps of the post office and the low wall around the war memorial. Ten years ago there were fourteen benches on the same stretch, the city removed them one by one, mostly after complaints that people lingered on them too long.

[2] We think that lingering is exactly the point. A bench gives an older resident a place to rest halfway to the pharmacy. It gives a parent somewhere to retie a child's shoe and a teenager somewhere to wait for a ride. Shop owners on Main Street told us, almost without exception, that customers who have room to rest stays longer and spends more.

[3] The objections deserve a fair hearing. Some merchants worry that benches will summon groups who block the sidewalk. Others point to the cost, a well-made bench with a back and armrests costs roughly what the city spends to repave a single parking space. For instance, neither concern outweighs the benefit, and both can be met. Benches can be set against building walls, where they leave the walkway clear, and a local foundation has offered to pay for the first ten.

[4] Benches also do something harder to measure. A person sitting on Main Street is a person watching Main Street, and a street with watchers tends to feel safer to everyone walking along it. The city's own planning office made this argument in a 2019 report on downtown safety, yet the benches never came back.

[5] We are not asking for a fountain or some ridiculously expensive plaza thing. We are asking the council to return a few places to sit, so that Main Street can again be a place to stay and not only a place to pass through.

PASSAGE P2: Slowing the Escape
=====================================================

[1] Pour hot coffee into an ordinary mug and it will be lukewarm within half an hour. Pour the same coffee into a vacuum flask and it can still be hot enough to steam the next morning. The flask adds no heat of its own. It simply slows the three ways heat escapes, conduction, convection, and radiation.

[2] A flask is really two bottles, one inside the other, joined only at the neck. Most of the air between the two walls have been pumped out. Conduction needs matter to carry heat from one molecule to the next, so the near-vacuum leaves the heat almost nothing to travel through. Convection, the movement of warm air or liquid, is blocked for the same reason, there is too little air in the gap to circulate.

[3] [A] Radiation is different. [B] Every warm object gives off infrared light, and infrared can cross a vacuum as easily as sunlight crosses space. [C] To slow this last escape route, the walls facing the gap are coated with a thin, mirrorlike layer of metal that reflects most of the infrared back toward the coffee. [D] With all three routes narrowed, the coffee can lose its heat only very slowly.

[4] No flask is perfect. Some heat still leaks through the neck, where the inner and outer bottles meet, and through the stopper which must be made of a material that conducts heat poorly and does not pass it along well, such as cork or plastic foam. In contrast, a flask filled only halfway cools faster then a full one: the same heat leaks out through the neck and stopper, but there is less coffee to lose it from.

[5] The same design works in reverse. Fill the flask with ice water, and the vacuum, together with the mirrored walls, keeps the warmth of the surrounding air from getting in. The flask, in other words, does not know hot from cold; it only slows the traffic between inside and out.

PASSAGE P3: Rings of Glass
=====================================================

[1] For most of history, a lighthouse was little more than a fire on a tower, and ships still ran aground within sight of shore on clear nights. By the late 1700s the fires had given way to oil lamps backed by polished metal reflectors, but even the best of these sent much of their light up into the sky or down into the sea, where no sailor at sea would ever see it.

[2] The solution came from a French engineer named Augustin Fresnel, who had been studying how light bends as it passes through glass. A single lens large enough to gather a lighthouse's beam would have been impossibly thick and heavy. Fresnel saw that most of a lens's bending happens at its curved surface, the glass behind that surface mostly just adds weight. He therefore proposed a lens built from rings of glass prisms, each ring angled to bend the light toward the horizon. The result was far thinner and lighter than a solid lens, yet it gathered nearly all of the lamp's light into a single beam.

[3] The first of these lenses was installed in 1823. It was installed at a lighthouse at the mouth of the Gironde River in France. Fresnel himself was often ill and spent part of his career supervising the construction of roads. Within a few decades, lighthouses from Scotland to the United States have been fitted with the new lenses. They were sorted into orders by size, the largest, a first-order lens, stood taller than a grown man. Smaller orders served harbors and rivers, where a light needed to carry only a few miles.

[4] For example, electric lamps replaced the oil flames, and in many places the keeper's job disappeared altogether. Many of the old lenses, however, is still in place, turning slowly above the water as they did two centuries ago. Visitors who climb a lighthouse today can often stand beside one and see the rings of glass that a single idea set in motion.

PASSAGE P4: Every Word in the Room
=====================================================

[1] At nine o'clock each weekday morning, Delphine Sato sits down at a small machine in front of the judge's bench and does not stop typing until the court breaks for lunch. She is a court reporter, and her job is to produce a word-for-word record of everything said in the room, every question, every answer, and every objection.

[2] Her machine looks nothing like a typewriter. It has only twenty-two keys, and Sato presses several of them at once, the way a pianist plays a chord, to spell out whole syllables or words in a single stroke. Each of the keys stand for a sound rather than a letter. Software turns the strokes into English as she goes, and a skilled reporter can write more than two hundred words a minute this way.

[3] The speed matters, but accuracy matters more. When two lawyers talk over each other, Sato must catch both, and when a witness mumbles, she will politely command the witness to repeat the answer. Each evening she reviews the days transcript, correcting strokes the software misread and adding the spellings of unusual names. It is slow, careful work, most people in the courtroom never see it.

[4] [A] Sato learned the job in a two-year program that only a fraction of its students finish. [B] Even now, before each trial, she writes out a list of the names and technical terms she expects to hear. [C] She calls the list her "brief," borrowing the lawyers' word for their own preparation. [D] The habit, she says, is the closest thing her work has to a rehearsal.

[5] Some courts have tried replacing reporters with audio recordings. In one county, therefore, a single week of recordings produced dozens of passages marked "inaudible." A microphone cannot ask a witness to speak up. It also cannot tell two voices apart when they overlap. For now, many courts still want a person in Sato's chair.

PASSAGE P5: Swing
=====================================================

[1] You arrive at the boathouse at six, before the sun has cleared the trees on the far bank, and the river is so still that it looks painted. Eight people you have never met are already at the dock, they are carrying a long, narrow boat down to the water on their shoulders. Someone hands you an oar that is taller than you are.

[2] The coach, a retired teacher named Augusta Vey, explains that rowing is less about strength than about timing.
