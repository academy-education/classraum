## Passage for items 1-4

Strand: Informational Text | Grade target: 8 | Target band: RIT 210-219

Ines Vargas had climbed to the limestone plateau above Sarnia Bay for six years before she admitted that she did not understand the plant she had come there to study. The shrub is a knee-high thing with grey leaves and pale, trumpet-shaped flowers, and it opens only after dark. It does not open every night. Her first notebooks record what looks like a rhythm — three nights shut, one night of open flowers — and for two seasons she took the plant to be keeping a calendar of its own.

Then the openings drew together into a tight cluster that arrived four days earlier the following month, breaking the rhythm outright. When she went back through six years of her own records, the pattern was plain: the flowers opened on nights when the moon lay within three nights of new and the plateau was black, and on no others.

A pattern is not a reason, so Vargas set out to learn who was visiting. She dusted the throats of forty flowers with fluorescent powder and walked the plateau after midnight with an ultraviolet lamp. The powder turned up on the bristled back of a wingless cricket that climbed to each flower from the ground, and on nothing else she caught that season.

The crickets are hunted by a small owl that quarters the plateau, and they will not climb an exposed stem on nights when moonlight shows them plainly. A flower opening under a full moon would be opening to nobody. Vargas has never seen an owl take a cricket from a stem, and next season she means to set call recorders above the shrubs and run them through bright nights and dark nights alike.

What she will claim is narrow, and she thinks it is more useful for being narrow. The plant is not counting nights. It is reading the same signal its one visitor reads, and it is reading it a few hours earlier.

## Passage for items 10-15

Strand: Literary Text | Grade target: 8 | Target band: RIT 210-219

The summer I turned fifteen my parents sent me to Kettle Cove to work for my great-aunt Sabine, who had grown oysters on a leased stretch of tidal flat there since before I was born. She met the bus without smiling and said that the working day began at low water. My work, from June to September, was the tally book: I wrote down the count of every rack on the flat, morning and evening. She did not put me to grading the young stock at the sorting board, or to mending and re-tarring the wire trays, or to rowing the skiff and reading the tide tables; those she kept for herself.

At low tide the flat was a grey field ribbed with racks. The red buoy at the mouth of the channel had been set there by her brother, and she painted it every spring so that the memory of him would stay in plain view. It was not a boundary marker, not a tide signal, and not a warning to strangers about the shoal; it was her brother, kept as near as she could keep him. Twice a week a truck came for the crates.

The word she used for what she wanted from me was check, and she asked for one every morning: the slow look over every tray in turn, one rack at a time. She did not mean a hairline split in a shell, or a written order for money owed, or anything that held the animals back from growing. I learned to answer her in a single word.

She took the largest orders the market offered, lifted racks whenever they were ready, and worked the far flat across the channel like any other ground; the one thing she refused was to let a hand she had not trained herself set foot on the flat. I asked her once whether that refusal had cost her anything, and she said it had. I respected her hands and thought her wrong about the flat, and both of those are still true. She never struck me as comic, I was never a cool observer keeping my distance, and my regard for her was never the unreserved kind.

Kestrel Falls ran three bus routes to an emptying town; after 2011 ridership climbed by half. Four explanations are offered around the council table: the fare cut to a dollar; the new route out to the county hospital; the parking charge brought in downtown; and the move of the high school to the north end. Ochoa's office weighed the four.

The counts are for the school move: half the new trips are made by students going north. The dollar fare began four years before the climb, and riding fell in each of those years. The hospital route was cut to one morning trip in 2009 and has carried almost nobody since. The parking charge downtown was voted down twice and has never been collected.

The service stops at a line that marks the county boundary on the map. No route in the town is called a line, and the office knows its three routes by number. No line of people stands waiting at the depot door, since riders scatter along the kerb. No line of printed words appears at the foot of the timetable, which carries figures only.

What the driver's log supplies is a weather note: each driver marks rain, ice or clear. No count of riders is marked in the log, and the depot adds no totals from it. No time of leaving is marked in the log, and no sheet of minutes is filed. No name is signed in the log, the depot assigning its buses by ticket instead.

The finding runs the other way too: moving the high school back would drop the riders away. Raising the fare above a dollar was tried in 2016, and nobody stopped riding. Closing the hospital route was tried in the winter of 2017, and nobody stopped riding. Lifting the parking charge was tried in 2018, and nobody stopped riding. The finding is proved and of small consequence: the town empties either way. I will not say flatly that the finding is proved, for another year of counts is wanted. Nor will I call the finding likely, since likely claims more than the counts carry. I doubt neither the method nor the counting behind it.

## Passage for items 22-27

Strand: Literary Text | Grade target: 8 | Target band: RIT 210-219

My uncle's boat stood in the shed above the beach for eleven years. Four answers were available to me that summer: that the hull was cracked; that he had promised my mother he would not sail; that the mooring fee had gone unpaid; and that the oars had been sold. I found out which in August.

The answer was the promise: my mother had asked him never to sail again, and he had given her his word. The hull was sound: a lamp under the bow showed no crack, and the planks were tight. The mooring fee was paid: the harbour office held no bill against his name. The oars hung on their pegs above the boat the whole time.

There is a hold to be had on the gunwale, a grip worn smooth by two hands. There is no hold under the foredeck and no locker of any kind. There is no hold I have on the matter and no opinion of my own, then or now. There is no hold left in the old rope, which will bear no weight.

What the letter on the shed door supplies is a name: the man it was written to stands at its head. No date stands at the head of the letter on the shed door. No sum stands in the letter on the shed door, which asks for no money. No instruction stands in the letter on the shed door.

The boat could have floated that August if my mother had freed him from his word. The planks were drawn together and pitched that June; the boat stayed in the shed. The harbour bill was paid off that June; the boat stayed in the shed. A pair of oars was bought that June; the boat stayed in the shed. What I felt for him then was anger, and I have felt nothing else since. Pity was not in it; I have never once been sorry for him. Relief was not in it; nothing at all was lifted from me by knowing. Impatience was not in it; I have never wanted to shake him.

## Passage for items 28-31

Strand: Informational Text | Grade target: 8 | Target band: RIT 210-219

Every March the pale salamanders of the Radna hills leave the limestone crevices where they have wintered and travel, some of them two hundred meters, to breed in shallow rock pools. For most of the last century the standard account held that each animal remembered its route. Teodora Vasnev, a field biologist who had spent eleven winters in the hills, thought the account did no work. Memory, she liked to say, was the name of the puzzle and not its solution.

In 1996 she built something that let her ask a narrower question. Four artificial pools, identical basins of poured concrete, were sunk into a level clearing forty meters from any natural water, at the corners of a square eight meters on a side. She labeled them A through D. Under pools A and B she set a small motor that tapped the basin twice a second, the rate of water dripping from stone; under pools C and D she set motors that never ran. Nothing else about the four differed: the same water, the same depth, the same rim of gravel. At dusk she carried ninety-six adults from three home crevices to the center of the square and left them there.

Averaged over eleven nights across two springs, sixty-one of the ninety-six were in A or B by dawn, twenty-two were still in the open, and thirteen were in C or D. The pattern held on nine nights of the eleven.

The untreated pools were the informative ones. Pool D took no animal at all until, on the last three nights, she shifted it three meters east toward the release point; it then took thirty a night, more than either tapping pool. Vasnev put this in the body of the paper rather than in a footnote.

In the second season she drew the path of every animal on a sheet of waxed paper laid over a map of the clearing, to learn whether the animals approached the pools directly or by searching. The lines ran straight from the release point and bent only in the last two meters.

She published in 1999, in a journal few outside the region read, and for a decade the work was cited mainly by people who studied cave fish. What makes it worth reading now is less the cue she found than the way she isolated it: she built a clearing in which only one question could be asked at a time, and then reported the one result that spoiled the answer.
