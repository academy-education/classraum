# sat-cs-wic-v26 author brief: Digital SAT Words in Context, all hard, FOUR-WORLD method

You write Digital SAT Reading & Writing **Words in Context** items with the four-world method. Read this whole
brief before you write anything. The pre-registration is `scripts/study-bank/PREREG-WIC26-2026-10-23.md`.

## The method, and what the last round taught
For each item you write:
- ONE set of four words;
- FOUR versions of the passage ("worlds").

In world k, word k is the only correct answer and the passage's logic rules out the other three. After every file
is frozen, a seeded random draw picks one world per item to serve. The other three worlds are never shown to anyone.
Every word is the answer in one of its worlds, so the four words alone say nothing about which world was served.

The last round (v25) used this method and passed: 21 of 32 items reached students. It still lost 11, and those
losses are why this brief has new rules:
- **5 items were too easy.** Before revision, readers rated 2 of 128 worlds hard. Most worlds were checklists: one
  planted fact per wrong word, then a "But..." clause that handed over the key. The fix came only in the revision
  round. This round it applies from the first draft (rule A below).
- **2 items used a word above the student's level:** the key "ancillary" and the distractor "lodestar" (rule B).
- **1 item had two near-opposite options:** "resilient" / "outmoded" (rule C).
- **3 items had a wrong word that fits the served passage literally:**
  - "interlude" for a short mid-play scene that advances nothing;
  - "pared" for bus routes whose long loops were cut;
  - "engine" for a newspaper that drove a movement's votes (rule D).
- **1 kept item had a blank sentence that presupposed something absent:** "Having compared the surviving drafts,"
  with no drafts anywhere in the passage (rule E).

**Each of the four worlds must be a real, hard, single-answer SAT item.** Any world might be the one served, and a
weak world is a weak item a quarter of the time. Graders see only the served world and judge it as an ordinary item.

## The five new rules (A-E)
- **A. One mechanism per world, told over two or more sentences.**
  - Each world describes ONE situation or process. The key follows from how its parts fit together.
  - The wrong words fail BECAUSE of that same mechanism, not because of separate facts added to rule them out.
  - **No kill-only clause.** Never write a clause whose only job is to contradict one wrong word (for example, "and
    the spit came back"). If deleting a clause would leave the mechanism intact and only revive one distractor,
    the clause is a planted kill.
  - **No pivot.** Do not open a sentence with "But", "Yet", "However", "Still" or "Even so", and do not use a
    ", but" / ", yet" / "; but" / "; yet" clause. The self-check refuses these. Rewording a pivot as "Even then" or
    "All the same" does not help: the world probe reads for the turn, not the word.
  - The test: can a strong student get the key from any ONE sentence? If yes, the world is not hard.
- **B. Every option is a word a strong 11th grader reads unglossed.** Picture a front-page news story or a
  textbook chapter that would use it without explaining it. "Ancillary" and "lodestar" failed that test with all
  three graders. When unsure, pick the more common word and make the passage do the work.
- **C. No two options that are near-opposites.** This includes words at the two ends of one scale (new vs.
  obsolete, durable vs. fragile, "resilient" vs. "outmoded"), not only dictionary antonyms. Graders name the pair,
  and a pair halves the field without the passage.
- **D. No world in which a wrong word literally describes what the passage reports.**
  - For each world, ask of every wrong word: is this a true description of something in this passage, even if the
    key is better?
  - "Interlude" is literally true of a short scene in the middle of a play. "Pared" is literally true of routes
    whose long loops were cut. "Engine" is literally true of a paper whose editorials set a committee's votes.
  - If the answer is yes, the item has two answers. Change the world's facts, not the explanation.
- **E. The blank sentence must not presuppose anything that some world lacks.**
  - The blank sentence is identical in all four worlds, so every noun phrase it takes for granted ("the surviving
    drafts", "the revised plan", "the second survey") must be set up in EVERY world.
  - If you cannot set it up naturally in all four, cut it from the blank sentence.

## How to build one item
1. **Frame.** Write the parts all four worlds share:
   - an opening sentence or two that sets the topic;
   - the **blank sentence**, which contains `______`.

   The blank sentence must be **word-for-word identical in all four worlds.** On its own, all four words must fit
   it grammatically and sensibly. Check rule E now.
2. **Four words.** Choose four words with these properties:
   - the same part of speech, and the same shape (all one word, or all two words);
   - each fits the blank sentence;
   - each could be the precise answer if the surrounding sentences said the right thing;
   - each passes rule B.

   No two may be opposites or near-opposites (rule C). No two may be near-synonyms that differ only in degree or
   scope ("stretch" vs "shift"). Those pairs cannot be separated cleanly, and graders will name a second answer.
3. **Four mechanisms.** For each word, write the situation that makes it the unique answer, over two or more
   sentences (rule A). These sentences are what changes between worlds; keep everything else the same where you
   can.
   - Each wrong word must be ruled out by something the reader has to COMBINE with the blank sentence: a timeline,
     a cause, a scope, a before-and-after, a purpose.
   - At most ONE wrong word per world may be ruled out by a single phrase that contradicts it outright
     (`explicit`). The others are ruled out by `logic`.
4. **Cross-check all sixteen pairs.** For every world, read each of the three wrong words into the blank and ask
   two questions:
   - Could a careful strong student defend this word here, even with a stretch?
   - Is this word literally true of something the passage reports (rule D)?

   If either answer is yes, rewrite that world until both are no. Exactly one word may be defensible in each
   world.
5. **Symmetry.** Do not write one "native" world and then bend the passage three times to fit the other words.
   - Every world must read as naturally as the others.
   - World lengths must stay within 1.3x of each other (checked).
   - Every pair of worlds must share at least 40% of its content words (checked). In practice: keep the opening and
     the blank sentence fixed, and change the mechanism sentences.

   A contorted world is a with-source tell, and contorted worlds are the ones graders drop. Dropping them would
   tilt the kept keys back toward the "natural" word. The pipeline measures that tilt and will hold the whole batch
   if it finds it.

## Examples: three v25 worlds that graders rated hard (their words are spent: the self-check refuses them)
Each block is the served world exactly as students see it. Notice what they share:
- one situation, told over several sentences;
- the key that names how the parts fit together;
- wrong words that fail because of that same situation.

**WIC25B-01** (graders: hard, hard, hard). Options: escalated / channeled / entrenched / deferred. Key: **channeled**.
> In 1838 a provincial assembly passed a law governing disputes between landowners and the tenant farmers who
> rented their fields. It created a county board as the only lawful place to contest a rent, and required the
> landowner to answer at the board's next sitting. Sheriffs' reports that had recorded dozens of blocked roads and
> burned hayricks in 1837 record almost none after 1839, while the board's minutes list hundreds of petitions a
> year, close to the number of incidents the sheriffs once counted, until both lists dwindle after grain prices
> fell. Looking back, one historian argued that the law had ______ the conflict it was written to end.

Why it works: the key needs three things together. Violence fell, petitions rose, and the two counts match. That
match is the mechanism: the same conflict moved into a new course. "Escalated" and "entrenched" fail on the same
counts. "Deferred" fails on the board's design ("answer at the board's next sitting").

**WIC25B-02** (graders: hard, hard, medium). Options: clearinghouse / battleground / archive / forum. Key:
**clearinghouse**.
> A sociologist spent three years observing the Saturday market of a small farming town, whose stalls sold much the
> same goods every week. The town had no newspaper, no telephone exchange, and no employment office. The sociologist
> found that farmers left word at particular stalls of jobs, strayed animals, and land to let, that the stallholders
> sorted these notices by village and handed each to the next customer from that village, and that most hired hands
> in the district had found their places this way. By the 1950s, the sociologist concluded, the market mattered to
> the town less for its trade than as its principal ______.

Why it works: one process (notices left, sorted, handed on) defines the key. "Forum" and "archive" are near-misses
that the same process rules out: the notices are routed, not debated, and passed on, not stored.

**WIC25A-01** (graders: medium, hard, hard). Options: starved / sheltered / stranded / swamped. Key: **sheltered**.
> Each spring flightless beetles nest on a gravel bar in a western river, eating the seeds of low plants there and
> walking across a narrow gravel spit to mate with colonies on the bank. This year the flood crested no higher than
> usual, and the spit came back. The water filled the burrows of the bank ants that cross the spit each summer to
> carry off larvae, no ants reached the bar all season, and in July it held more young than in any year of the
> survey. After surveying the bar in July, one ecologist reported that the year's flood had ______ the colony.

Why it works, and where it falls short: the key needs a chain of three links (flood, then drowned predators, then
record young). But "and the spit came back" is a kill-only clause. It exists only to rule out "stranded", and under
rule A it would not be allowed. One grader rated this world medium, and that clause is the likely reason.

## The v25 frame, kept for its structure (its words are spent: outstayed / reflected / parceled / rerouted)
Shared opening: "In 1612 the town council issued a charter regulating the weavers who sold cloth in its market."
Shared blank sentence: "Writing two centuries later, one historian concluded that the charter had ______ the
trade it was drafted to govern."

Each world then described a different fate for the trade:
- it died while the charter lived on (outstayed);
- the charter wrote down existing practice (reflected);
- the charter split the trade between two groups (parceled);
- the charter's tax moved the trade elsewhere (rerouted).

The v25 brief showed planted-phrase deciding sentences for these worlds. Those are exactly the checklist pattern
rule A now forbids, so they are not reproduced here.

## Rules (the self-check enforces the starred ones)
- \* **Four worlds, four different keys.**
  - Each world names its `key`.
  - The four keys are the four `choices`, each used once.
  - A world that reuses another world's key is refused.
- \* **Identical blank sentence.**
  - `blank_sentence` must appear verbatim in every world's passage.
  - No two items may share a blank sentence.
- \* **Kills come from outside the blank sentence.**
  - For every world, list a kill for each of its three wrong words, with:
    - `type`: `logic` or `explicit`;
    - `quote`: 2+ consecutive words of THAT world's passage, lying outside the blank sentence;
    - `why`.
  - At most one `explicit` per world.
  - The quote shows WHERE the reader finds the reason. It is not a license to plant a clause for it (rule A).
- \* **No pivot** (rule A): no sentence opening with But / Yet / However / Still / Even so, and no ", but" / ", yet"
  / "; but" / "; yet" clause.
- \* **Letters.** Every option word starts with a letter in YOUR range:

  | Author | Letters |
  |---|---|
  | A | o-r |
  | B | s-z |
  | C | a-c |
  | D | d-h |
  | E | i-n |

  The ranges are rotated from v25, so no author works in letters it held last round. They are unequal in width
  because they are balanced on supply: each holds 15-25% of the initials of past WIC words.
- \* **Exclusion.** The self-check refuses any word that is:
  - a live WIC word;
  - any v17-v25 option word (key or distractor, kept or dropped, served or unserved), or a stem or inflection of
    one;
  - a word another author used;
  - a denylisted word.
- \* **No opposites.** `antonym_pairs: []`, and mean it, including near-opposites (rule C).
- \* **Shape.**
  - Every item `difficulty: "hard"`.
  - Every world has `key_from_one_sentence: false`.
  - Each world declares its `shape`: cause and mechanism, comparison of two cases, before and after, scope limit,
    method and purpose, process sequence, definition by example, ...
  - Use at least 4 distinct shapes across your 32 worlds.
  - "Received view overturned" may appear in at most 25% of your worlds.
- \* **Passages.**
  - Each world is 50-110 words, with exactly one blank of six underscores.
  - No "a" or "an" directly before the blank.
  - `after_blank` names the word right after the blank. It must read idiomatically with all four words.
  - Use invented or no proper names (prefer a role: "one historian"), never reused across items.
  - American punctuation and double quotes.
- **Word level** (rule B). The four words should be about equally common.
  - Declare `rarity_order` (most common first). It is not scored per author. It is used to check, after grading,
    that the kept keys are not skewed toward common or rare words.
- **Nouns and verbs.** At least 3 of your 8 option sets are nouns or verbs (checked). A common word used in a
  precise sense makes a good hard key.
- \* **Explanation per world.**
  - 2-4 sentences.
  - Quote the key, and say why each of the other three words fails, naming each by its word.
  - Never refer to an option by a letter or by "option B".

## File format: ONE file per item
Path: `scripts/study-bank/sat-cs-wic-v26-work/<L>/WIC26<L>-NN.json`, with NN = 01..08.

```json
{
  "id": "WIC26A-01",
  "domain": "Craft and Structure",
  "subskill": "Words in Context",
  "difficulty": "hard",
  "topic_tag": "short topic",
  "prompt": "Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?",
  "choices": ["w1", "w2", "w3", "w4"],
  "antonym_pairs": [],
  "rarity_order": ["most common", "...", "...", "rarest"],
  "pos": "verb",
  "after_blank": "the",
  "blank_sentence": "<identical in all four worlds, contains ______>",
  "worlds": [
    {
      "key": "w1",
      "passage": "<opening> <mechanism sentences for w1> <blank_sentence>",
      "shape": "comparison of two cases",
      "key_from_one_sentence": false,
      "explanation": "... \"w1\" ... \"w2\" fails because ...; \"w3\" ...; \"w4\" ...",
      "kills": {
        "w2": {"type": "logic", "quote": "two or more words from this passage", "why": "..."},
        "w3": {"type": "logic", "quote": "...", "why": "..."},
        "w4": {"type": "explicit", "quote": "...", "why": "..."}
      }
    },
    {"key": "w2", "...": "..."},
    {"key": "w3", "...": "..."},
    {"key": "w4", "...": "..."}
  ]
}
```

The blank sentence does not have to come last. It may sit anywhere, as long as it is identical in all four worlds.
Choice order does not matter, because it is re-dealt after selection.

## Loop
After EACH file, run:

    cd /Users/andylee/Downloads/saas/classraum && node scripts/study-bank/wic26-selfcheck.mjs <L>

Fix every PROBLEM on your own items. The per-author count rules fire once you have 8 files. A `warn` line means
the exact span that kills a word in one world also appears in the world where that word is the key. That can be
legitimate, if the kill depends on another sentence too, but look at it.

Then, reading each world as a student who has never seen the other three:
- do the sixteen-pair cross-check (step 4) once more;
- check rules A-E.

## After all 8: the world probe and the ONE revision round
When every author has 8 green files, four fresh readers each read one world of every item and rate it. You then
get `sat-cs-wic-v26-work/<L>/PROBE-FEEDBACK.json`, listing each named world with the reader's difficulty, flags and
note.
- **Every world the reader rated easy must be rewritten.** The note says what gave it away. A world rated easy and
  left byte-identical blocks the freeze for the whole batch (`wic26-wprobe.mjs verify`).
- Fix every named "second defensible", "distractor fits literally" and "presupposes absent" world.
- A revision rewrites a world. It never changes the world's key and never removes a world.
- After the revision round the text is frozen, whatever the outcome. There is no second round.

## Process rules
- Write 4 items per run (the run brief says which numbers). Then stop and report:
  - the self-check line;
  - the three WORLDS (item id + key) you consider weakest, and why.
- NEVER run `wic26-select.mjs`, `wic26-wprobe.mjs` or any render script, and never try to work out which world
  will be served. The seed depends on the frozen text, so you cannot know which world will be served. Make all
  four worlds equally good.
- Never touch another author's folder or any file outside your own folder.
- NEVER run pkill, killall, or any command that kills processes.
- No GPT or other non-Claude model. Do not call any model API.
