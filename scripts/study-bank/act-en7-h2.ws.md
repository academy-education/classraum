PASSAGE P7: The Long Clock of the Bamboo
=====================================================

[1] Most flowering plants bloom on a calendar set by the seasons. A few bamboo species keep a different clock. One timber bamboo grown across East Asia flowers roughly once every 120 years, and when it does, nearly every plant in the world's scattered stands flower within a year or two of the others, whether it grows on a hillside in China or in a botanical garden on another continent.

[2] For centuries the pattern puzzled botanists, because the plants seem to have no way of signaling one another across oceans. Most researchers now think the timing is internal: each plant carries a kind of counter that began running when its parent stand last flowered. Bamboo spreads mainly through underground stems, so the plants in a forest are often clones of a single ancestor. They share a starting date. Nevertheless, they reach the end of the count together.

[3] What follows the flowering is as dramatic as the flowering itself. A stand produces an enormous crop of seed then it dies, often across hundreds of square miles at once. In northeastern India, one species flowers about every 48 years, and the flood of seed sets off a population explosion among rats. When the seed is gone, the rats turn to the farmers rice fields, and famine has followed several of these flowerings.

[4] Why would a plant wait so long and then spend everything at once? The most widely accepted explanation, first proposed by an ecologist in the 1970s, is that the strategy overwhelms the animals that eat seeds. If a bamboo released a little seed every year, mice, birds, and wild pigs could scarf down pretty much all of it. By flooding the ground with more seed than any population of seed-eaters can consume the plants ensure that some seeds survive to sprout.

[5] The long gap does its own work. Waiting decades kept the seed-eaters scarce between flowerings, so no large population is standing ready when the next crop arrives. A bamboo that flowered every few years would be feeding its enemies; one that waits a century is ambushing them.

=====================================================

[1] Before photography, a family that wanted a likeness of someone it loved had few choices. [2] The method was simple. The sitter sat sideways beside a candle, and the shadow of his or her head fell on a sheet of paper pinned to the wall. [3] Silhouettes were sold in many places. Cutters set up booths at county fairs, on seaside piers, and in the lobbies of spa hotels. A visitor could pay a few pennies, wait while the scissors moved, and walk away with a profile in an envelope.

Doubling the black sheet so that a single pass of the scissors produced two identical profiles.
